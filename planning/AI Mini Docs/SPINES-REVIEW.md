## The Rosewood dinner

**My read (v2).** The fix worked on scope. Story time is now the summer dinner to OpenAI's launch in December. The aftermath is one clause (the lawsuit), and the closing question comes from the climax ("Would OpenAI exist without Musk?"). **Two problems remain.**
1. **Hassabis became a title again** ("Google DeepMind's chief"). The spine dodged naming him, which is the v2 error in a new place.
2. **S5 uses Amodei's choice as the crisis, but it doesn't cause Sutskever's choice.** That's a weak link. The spine also admits a competing account: Altman says *he* recruited Sutskever.

- **Story question:** Will Elon Musk pull Ilya Sutskever away from Google to anchor a lab built to counter Google?
- **Starts / ends:** The Rosewood Sand Hill dinner, summer 2015 → OpenAI launches as a nonprofit, December 2015
- **Protagonist:** Elon Musk. Wants: To win Google's own researcher, Ilya Sutskever, for a new lab that will counter Google in AI Stakes: Without its star scientist the lab he is betting on is empty, and AI stays in the hands of the company he fears
- **Opposition:** Google. Wants: To keep top AI talent like Sutskever, backed by an offer of about $6 million a year Motive: Its dominance in AI, which Musk fears it will lock in (stated in S1)
- **Named:** Elon Musk (Tesla and SpaceX boss who fears Google's grip on AI, central); Sam Altman (Y Combinator president who wants to start an AI lab); Greg Brockman (ex-Stripe CTO at the dinner); Ilya Sutskever (Google researcher, the prize at the table, central); Dario Amodei (researcher Musk also invited)

**The plot**

1. *world* — In 2015 Elon Musk fears Google will lock in control of AI, and Sam Altman wants to start a lab of his own.  
   ↳ Google unchallenged → two men planning a challenge
2. *inciting* — Therefore they gather Greg Brockman, Ilya Sutskever and others at the Rosewood Sand Hill, where Musk opens by asking whether Google DeepMind's chief is evil.  
   ↳ an idea → a table of recruits facing Musk's crusade against Google
3. *complication* — But Sutskever is a Google researcher, and Google puts an offer of about $6 million a year in front of him.  
   ↳ willing recruit → a prize Google is paying to keep
4. *midpoint* — Therefore Musk takes on recruiting Sutskever himself, pitching a lab built to counter Google.  
   ↳ open invitation → Musk's personal pursuit
5. *crisis* — But Dario Amodei, whom Musk also invited, weighs joining and goes to Google Brain instead, leaving Sutskever to choose between Google's millions and Musk.  
   ↳ momentum for the lab → Google winning the talent fight
6. *climax* — Therefore Sutskever walks away from Google's offer, saying the chance to work with Musk was enough.  
   ↳ Google's money ahead → Musk's pull wins the prize
7. *resolution* — Therefore OpenAI launches that December as a nonprofit with Musk and Altman co-chairs, though Musk later sues Altman for 'stealing a charity'.  
   ↳ a dinner-table plan → a founded lab, and allies who later meet in court

- **Turn:** S6, planted in S2
- **Close question:** Would OpenAI exist without Musk?
- **Record lacks:** The record holds no dilemma for Musk himself; the crisis is Sutskever's choice (Google's offer or Musk), which is documented only through his 2026 testimony (F6) · Google has no named person with a stated motive; the opposition is the institution and its offer, with the motive taken from Musk's fear of Google's dominance (F3) · No dates exist for Google's offer or for Sutskever's decision; placing it after Amodei's October 2015 move to Google Brain is an ordering by consequence, not a confirmed date · Amodei's invitation rests on one source (F11, single_source); his attendance at the dinner is unconfirmed, and the writer must credit it on screen · The dinner's month is disputed, July or August 2015 (F2); the spine says summer 2015 · There is no record of what Musk said to Sutskever while recruiting him; S4 rests only on Musk being 'pivotal' (F6) · Altman's account (F8) says he recruited Sutskever himself, which competes with Musk's role; the reel should not present Musk as the only recruiter

## Best on Paper

**My read (v2).** This is the cleanest of the three. It covers about ten weeks with one question answered by the verdict, and the turn is planted in S3. The closing question ("Should numbers or people judge chatbots?") comes straight from the climax, and people can argue it. The crisis is still pressure, not a hard choice, and the record holds no such choice.

- **Story question:** Will Hugging Face's chatbot, first on every automatic metric, still win ConvAI2 once humans judge it?
- **Starts / ends:** Hugging Face enters ConvAI2, autumn 2018 → 9 Dec 2018, Montreal: Hugging Face finishes second
- **Protagonist:** Thomas Wolf. Wants: Win the ConvAI2 grand prize at NeurIPS 2018 with Hugging Face's chatbot Stakes: His small chatbot startup's claim to have built the best conversational AI in the world, made in public at the field's biggest conference
- **Opposition:** Lost in Conversation (Sergey Golovanov and Alexander Tselousov). Wants: Win the human-evaluation grand prize ($20,000 in Mechanical Turk funding) Motive: They ranked only sixth on the automatic metrics and barely reached the finals, so the human round, where the grand prize is decided, is their only path to winning
- **Named:** Thomas Wolf (Hugging Face researcher, co-author of its entry, central); Hugging Face (Small chatbot startup entering the contest, central); Lost in Conversation (Sergey Golovanov and Alexander Tselousov) (Two-person team that barely made the finals, central); ConvAI2 organizers (Dinan, Logacheva, Weston, Burtsev et al.) (Contest organizers who set the rules)

**The plot**

1. *world* — Thomas Wolf and his colleagues at chatbot startup Hugging Face enter ConvAI2, a NeurIPS contest, to prove their bot talks best.  
   ↳ small chatbot startup → contender for state of the art
2. *inciting* — Therefore they fine-tune a pretrained model, and when results lock, Hugging Face tops every automatic metric while Lost in Conversation scrapes into the finals sixth.  
   ↳ contender → clear favourite; rival → long shot
3. *complication* — But the ConvAI2 organizers award the grand prize on human ratings, not metrics, giving Lost in Conversation, a two-person team, one last shot.  
   ↳ secure lead → a lead that doesn't decide the prize
4. *crisis* — Therefore paid Mechanical Turk workers chat with each finalist, and Hugging Face's bot opens 107 replies in 100 chats with a question; humans did 12.  
   ↳ the model's strength → a hidden flaw in front of the judges
5. *climax* — But on 9 December in Montreal, the organizers name Lost in Conversation the winner, rated 3.11 to Hugging Face's 2.68.  
   ↳ favourite → beaten; long shot → champion
6. *resolution* — Therefore the best bot on paper finishes second, and five months later Wolf open-sources its code, cut from 3,000 lines to 250.  
   ↳ prize-seeking product → second place, its code given away

- **Turn:** S5, planted in S3
- **Close question:** Should numbers or people judge chatbots?
- **Record lacks:** Lost in Conversation's own stated motive or actions during the contest: the sources record only their ranking and result, so their motive is inferred from the contest structure (sixth place, human-judged grand prize) · A crisis dilemma: the record holds no choice Wolf or Hugging Face faced between two goods or two evils before the verdict; S4 is pressure without a decision · Wolf's or Hugging Face's reaction on 9 December: not documented · A causal link between the loss and the code release: the sources show the release happened (Hugging Face's own README and blog, single source), not that the loss caused it · Story time runs about ten weeks (automatic results locked 30 Sep to the 9 Dec verdict), plus one aftermath event in May 2019; no shorter stretch holds both the leaderboard and the human verdict

## Thirty-Two GPUs Against Sora

**My read (v2).** It's tighter (13 months instead of 2½ years) and now ends at the desktop relaunch, but it still runs longer than the episode rule allows, and it says so. The new pitch gate rejected this pitch outright: the record has no opponent who acts. **I recommend dropping it as a test pitch.**

- **Story question:** Will Alex Mashrabov's 32-GPU video model win ordinary creators, and keep them, while OpenAI keeps Sora locked away?
- **Starts / ends:** February 2024: OpenAI unveils Sora but keeps it gated → March 31, 2025: Higgsfield relaunches on desktop for professionals
- **Protagonist:** Alex Mashrabov. Wants: Get ordinary creators making videos in his own app, Diffuse, built on his team's own model, while Sora is still closed to the public Stakes: The company he and Yerzat Dulat founded, its $8 million seed, and his public claim that 16 people on 32 GPUs can stand up to OpenAI
- **Opposition:** OpenAI. Wants: Keep Sora under control as a gated research preview, not a public product Motive: Concern about how a realistic video model could be abused
- **Named:** Alex Mashrabov (Higgsfield co-founder and CEO, ex-head of generative AI at Snap, central); Yerzat Dulat (Co-founder, AI researcher from Kazakhstan); Higgsfield (Their small AI video startup); OpenAI (Maker of Sora, running on tens of thousands of GPUs, central)

**The plot**

1. *world* — Alex Mashrabov wants ordinary creators making AI video; his startup Higgsfield, with Yerzat Dulat, has trained its own model on just 32 GPUs.  
   ↳ no product → a model of their own, ready to ship
2. *inciting* — But OpenAI, which uses tens of thousands of GPUs, unveils Sora and keeps it from the public, citing the risk of abuse.  
   ↳ open field → a giant enters the race but leaves the door shut
3. *complication* — Therefore, seven weeks later, Mashrabov raises $8 million and launches Diffuse, a phone app that turns anyone's selfie into a generated clip.  
   ↳ hidden lab → public challenger to Sora
4. *midpoint* — But the abuse risk that kept Sora locked hangs over his selfie app too, so he holds back: cartoon-style clips, a few English-speaking markets first.  
   ↳ racing for reach → choosing restraint over realism and reach
5. *complication* — Therefore Diffuse spreads market by market and reaches a million users in two months.  
   ↳ cautious test → apparent hit
6. *climax* — But retention is weak: the users who came to make selfie clips don't stay.  
   ↳ a million users won → a win that won't hold
7. *resolution* — Therefore Mashrabov takes Higgsfield off the phone, relaunching on desktop for professional creators and marketers.  
   ↳ casual selfie app → professional tool

- **Turn:** S6, planted in S3
- **Close question:** Was the model ever the real product?
- **Record lacks:** No opponent acts against Higgsfield directly · The crisis dilemma isn't where the structure wants it · Story time runs about 13 months, longer than the target · No retention numbers exist
