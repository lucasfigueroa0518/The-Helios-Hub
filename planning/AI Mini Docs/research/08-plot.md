# 08 · Plot: the spine writer's and the story editor's brain

Desk research for Iteration 3 (MD-66 to MD-73): one bounded episode per reel, a SPINE written before the
lines, and a STORY EDITOR that reads the script cold and repairs it. Compiled 2026-11-02.

**How to read a line.** `P-n Do X, because Y. [source · grade] [roles]`
- **Grades:** **E** evidence (a study with data). **P** professional practice from an established craft
  authority. **F** creator folklore. **I** my inference for the 60-second format, built from the cited sources.
- **Roles:** **[spine]** the writer's first pass, 5–7 plain sentences that are the plot. **[lines]** the writer
  turning the spine into narration. **[editor]** the story editor's diagnosis and repair.
- **Cross-references.** Research files 01, 02, 03 and 05 have no ids of their own; they are cited as
  `02 §7` (file, section). writer-craft.md's `W-`/`D-`/`S-` ids are cited where that file uses them.
  This file does not repeat a principle those files already state; it points to them.
- **Altitude.** No AI-industry examples. The one worked example (§2) is about a non-AI subject and is

## 1. Principles by topic

### 1.1 Scope: one action, one question, one end point

- **P-1 Make the reel the imitation of one action, with a beginning, middle and end that belong to it,** because Aristotle's unity of action says a plot is one whole action whose parts can't be moved or removed without breaking it; a life or an era is not a plot. [1 · P] [spine][editor]
- **P-2 State the story question as "Will [protagonist] [get/stop/prove X]?" and let it decide where the reel starts and ends,** because the dramatic question is raised by the inciting incident and answered by the climax; whatever falls outside that span is backstory or aftermath, not story. [3, 5, 12 · P] [spine][editor]
- **P-3 Enter as late as the question allows and leave as soon as it is answered,** because the screenwriting rule "come in late, get out early" (Goldman) trims setup and denouement, the two places a short sags. [16 · P] [spine][editor]
- **P-4 Separate story time from backstory and aftermath, and give each of the latter one line at most,** because Bernard distinguishes the story's own timeframe (the train) from backstory that rides in only when needed; 01 §2's Tiger King frame is the same idea. MD-66 sets the one-line cap. [12, 22 · P] [spine][editor]
- **P-5 Test scope by deletion: if a beat can be cut without the climax losing its cause, it is out of scope,** because Aristotle's test of a part's membership is that removing it changes the whole. [1 · P/I] [editor]
- **P-6 Prefer a scaled-down episode (one decisive night, meeting or week) over a saga,** because 05 §7 already recommends the smaller version, and a short can dramatize one turn but can only list many. [05 §7 · P/I] [spine]

### 1.2 Dramatic structure, compressed

- **P-7 Build on the five load-bearing parts: inciting incident, progressive complications, crisis, climax, resolution,** because McKee and Coyne (the Story Grid's "five commandments") find these in every working unit of story, from a beat to a whole book. [3, 8 · P] [spine][editor]
- **P-8 Read Freytag's pyramid (exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, catastrophe/dénouement) as a tension curve, not a timeline split into equal fifths,** because Freytag described five-act tragedy; modern practice places the climax near the end and keeps falling action very short. [2, 3 · P] [spine]
- **P-9 Make the inciting incident upset the protagonist's balance and force a want,** because McKee defines it as the event that radically disturbs the balance of forces in the protagonist's life and raises the question. [3 · P] [spine][editor]
- **P-10 Make each complication harder than the last and close a way back,** because McKee's progressive complications escalate past a point of no return; a complication that could be undone is not progress. [3 · P] [spine][editor]
- **P-11 Put a crisis before the climax: a dilemma in which the protagonist must choose between two goods or two evils,** because McKee and Coyne locate the story's meaning in that choice under pressure; true character shows there. [3, 8 · P] [spine]
- **P-12 Make the climax the action that answers the story question, absolutely and irreversibly,** because McKee requires the climax to be a change that cannot be taken back. [3 · P] [spine][editor]
- **P-13 Keep the resolution to the new balance the climax created, in one or two lines,** because Truby's "new equilibrium" and McKee's resolution both show the world after the change, then stop. [3, 4 · P] [spine][lines]
- **P-14 Use two hinges, not one: an early lock-in (the protagonist commits) and a late turn (expectation reverses),** because Field's two plot points and Seger's two turning points are what give a story momentum between beginning and end. [5, 15 · P] [spine]
- **P-15 Place a midpoint that raises the stakes or flips the protagonist from reacting to acting,** because Snyder and Field use the midpoint (false victory or false defeat) to stop the middle sagging. In 60s it is one line at roughly 25–35s. [6, 5 · P/I] [spine]
- **P-16 Compress to these windows (inference, not measured):** hook + protagonist + question 0–3s; inciting incident by ~8s (or already happened at the point of attack); complications 8–40s; midpoint ~30s; crisis ~40–48s; climax ~45–55s; resolution in the last line; close question over the brand close. [I] [spine]
- **P-17 Map 5–7 spine sentences to parts, not to rungs:** 1 world + want, 2 inciting incident, 3–4 complications (one may be the midpoint), 5 crisis, 6 climax, 7 resolution. A sentence may take one or two rungs. [10, 11, 3 · I] [spine][lines]

### 1.3 Conflict

- **P-18 Give the protagonist a concrete, visible want with a yes/no outcome,** because Field's "dramatic need" and Truby's "desire" must be specific enough that the viewer knows when it is won or lost. [5, 4 · P] [spine]
- **P-19 Give the opposition a face and a want of its own, ideally competing for the same prize,** because Truby's best opponent wants the same goal; a faceless "obstacle" produces no scene. [4 · P] [spine][editor]
- **P-20 Make the opposition's motive known to the viewer before it acts,** because an action without a motive reads as random, the fault Lucas named ("you haven't told the viewer why he is upset"). [3, 7 · P/I] [spine][editor]
- **P-21 Escalate conflict through steps, never jumps,** because Egri calls conflict that skips its transitions "jumping" and conflict that never moves "static"; only "rising" conflict holds. [7 · P] [spine][editor]
- **P-22 Foreshadow the conflict at the start,** because Egri's "foreshadowing conflict" promises the clash from the opening so tension exists before it arrives. [7 · P] [spine][lines]
- **P-23 Name the stakes as a loss the viewer can picture, before the danger peaks,** because suspense needs a feared outcome the viewer already understands (02 §8, 01 §1 on human cost). [14, 19 · P/E] [spine]
- **P-24 Use the conflict level the record supports: inner (doubt), personal (two people), or extra-personal (institution, law, market),** because McKee's three levels each produce different scenes; personal conflict between two documented people is the strongest at 60s. [3 · P/I] [spine]
- **P-25 Use a ticking clock only when the record has a real one,** because a deadline turns every beat into a countdown, but an invented one breaks 01 §12. [3, 01 §12 · P] [spine]
- **P-26 Orchestrate the cast as contrasts,** because Egri's orchestration sets characters against each other in temperament and aim, which makes their meeting a conflict by itself. [7 · P] [spine]

### 1.4 Causality

- **P-27 Make every event happen because of the one before it, not merely after it,** because Aristotle's propter hoc / post hoc distinction is the original form of Parker and Stone's but/therefore rule (02 §7). [1, 9 · P] [spine][editor]
- **P-28 Show the cause on screen before the effect,** because an effect with an unseen cause (a lawsuit with no reason) is an orphan event the viewer can't follow. [1, 3 · P/I] [spine][editor]
- **P-29 Write the spine with Adams's story spine as the causal skeleton (once upon a time / every day / one day / because of that / because of that / until finally / ever since then), then add opposition and cost,** because the skeleton guarantees causation but carries no drama; it has no opponent, no dilemma and no stakes. [10 · P/I] [spine]
- **P-30 Draft each spine sentence as an action with a subject and a verb, short enough to say in one breath,** because Franklin outlines a true story as one complication, three developments and one resolution, each a few words of action. [11 · P] [spine]
- **P-31 Make every beat turn a value (trust to distrust, safe to exposed, ally to rival),** because McKee and Coyne treat a scene with no value change as a non-event; that is the measurable test of "something happened". [3, 8 · P] [spine][editor]
- **P-32 Alternate the turns: a "but" (against the want) then a "therefore" (the protagonist's response),** because a chain of only "therefore" is a plan going well and a chain of only "but" is a list of misfortunes. [9 · P/I] [spine][editor]
- **P-33 Give each person a reason for each choice the reel shows,** because motivation is causality applied to people; a choice without a reason looks like a plot convenience, and in nonfiction like a slur. [3, 01 §10 · P/I] [spine][editor]

### 1.5 Exposition and introducing people

- **P-34 Turn exposition into ammunition: deliver a fact as something a person uses, risks or fights over,** because McKee's rule converts information into action, so it moves the train instead of stopping it. [3 · P] [lines]
- **P-35 Deliver context just in time: the beat before it is needed, and after the question that makes it wanted,** because Bernard's exposition-when-needed (01 §2) and the curiosity window (03 §3) together set its timing. [12 · P] [lines][editor]
- **P-36 Withhold, don't explain,** because McKee holds that interest comes from the information you hold back, not from what you give. [3 · P] [lines]
- **P-37 Introduce every person, at first appearance, with name, role and want in one line, while their face is on screen,** because screenwriting introduces a character with name and essence on first entry, Huppert wants their eyes (02 §3), and a person who acts before being introduced is noise. [3, 6, 02 §3 · P] [spine][lines][editor]
- **P-38 Introduce the protagonist doing something that shows what they want,** because Snyder's "save the cat" principle defines a character by a first action, not a description. [6 · P] [lines]
- **P-39 Name at most three people; everyone else is a role ("his co-founder", "the board")** because working memory holds about four chunks, and 02 §1 caps developed characters at one or two. [18, 13 · E/P] [spine][editor]
- **P-40 Call each person by one name throughout, and never by a title alone,** because a new label reads as a new person to the ear (02 §2, repeat the key noun). [02 §2 · P] [lines][editor]
- **P-41 Never use a pronoun, time reference or event the reel hasn't set up,** because writer.md's HARD 8 states it and Rosewood v2 broke it ("goes back to the dinner without mentioning the dinner"). [I] [lines][editor]

### 1.6 Subplots

- **P-42 Default to no subplot in 60 seconds,** because a subplot needs its own setup, turn and payoff (Seger), and a minute has room for one arc. [15 · P/I] [spine]
- **P-43 Allow one B-line only when it is the opposition's or an ally's own want, and it collides with the A-line at the climax,** because Jon Else's two-stories test (05 §1) and Seger's rule that a subplot must intersect the main plot both make it part of the conflict. [15, 05 §1 · P] [spine]
- **P-44 Give a B-line one of McKee's jobs (contradict the main line for irony, resonate with it, or complicate it); complicate is the strongest,** because a subplot that merely runs alongside splits the story in two. [3 · P] [spine][editor]
- **P-45 Let the B-line carry the turn when it can,** because Snyder's B story often delivers the insight that solves the A story; at 60s, a figure who opposes and then reverses is the subplot. [6 · P/I] [spine]
- **P-46 Cut any B-line the editor can delete without changing the climax,** because by P-5 it is out of scope. [1 · I] [editor]

### 1.7 Edge-of-seat mechanics

- **P-47 Choose the information state for each beat on purpose: suspense (viewer knows a possible outcome, not whether), curiosity (viewer knows an outcome, not the cause) or surprise (viewer learns something withheld),** because Brewer and Lichtenstein found each structure produces a distinct feeling. Hitchcock's bomb (02 §8) is the suspense case. [19, 14 · E/P] [spine][lines]
- **P-48 Make the viewer care before you make them worry,** because suspense needs both a liked or understood person and an uncertain outcome; danger to a stranger produces no suspense. [14, 3 · P] [spine]
- **P-49 Use dramatic irony: let the viewer know what a character doesn't, from the record,** because the gap between what we know and what they do is sustained tension (02 §8). [14, 3 · P] [spine]
- **P-50 Build the turn as a reversal that is surprising and, looking back, inevitable,** because Aristotle's peripeteia works best when it arises from what came before, and recognition (anagnorisis) gives it meaning. [1 · P] [spine][editor]
- **P-51 Turn the expected answer, not the question,** because a reversal that changes what the story is about breaks unity of action (P-1); one that flips the answer the viewer expected deepens it. [1, 3 · I] [spine][editor]
- **P-52 Keep a loop open across every cut,** because 02 §8 cycles question and answer; at the spine level that means each sentence answers the last and leaves a "so what will he do?" [02 §8 · P/I] [spine]
- **P-53 Raise the cost at each step, to the protagonist personally,** because escalation (02 §7) is felt only when the price lands on a person, not an institution (01 §1). [3 · P] [spine]

### 1.8 Documentary specifics

- **P-54 Pitch and build a story, not a topic: a person, a want, a struggle and an outcome,** because Bernard and Hart both draw this line (02 §1, 05 §1); a reel about "the dinner" is a topic until someone in it wants something. [12, 13 · P] [spine][editor]
- **P-55 Find the arc in the record by selection; never manufacture a beat the record doesn't hold,** because Bernard's drama comes from arrangement (02 §1), and 01 §12 forbids reordering that makes meaning. If the record has no crisis, the spine has no crisis. [12 · P] [spine][editor]
- **P-56 Choose the point of attack from the story question, not from the best photo,** because 02 §1 treats the entry point as the most consequential choice, and MD-68 already says assets follow choices. [12 · P/I] [spine]
- **P-57 When the material is more explanation than action, use Hart's explanatory narrative: one line of action with brief context digressions,** because forcing an arc onto thin action fails (02 §1); the spine then records "no crisis in the record" rather than inventing one. [13 · P] [spine]
- **P-58 Plan picture and line together for the beat that carries each spine sentence,** because Johnny Harris reportedly writes script and visual notes side by side (02 §12); only an action can be shown. [02 [42] · F] [spine][lines]

### 1.9 Resolution and endings

- **P-59 Close the story question; leave open only a consequence it created,** because McKee's closed ending answers the question absolutely, and his open ending leaves one or two questions while the emotion resolves; MD-05 asks for both. [3 · P] [spine][editor]
- **P-60 Pay every setup and set up every payoff,** because Chekhov's gun forbids a planted detail that never fires, and a payoff with no setup feels arbitrary. [17 · P] [spine][editor]
- **P-61 End on an image or act, not an explanation,** because 01 §4 already says so; Snyder adds that the final image should answer the opening image. [6 · P] [lines]
- **P-62 Derive the close question from the resolution line, as the question that answer creates,** because a springboard (01 §4) must spring from the landing; a question about another thread is a new story. [I] [spine][editor]
- **P-63 Test the close question by reading only the last line and the question aloud,** because if the question doesn't follow from that line alone, the viewer won't make the jump either. [I] [editor]

### 1.10 Story editing

- **P-64 Read the script cold before reading the pitch or spine,** because a story editor's value is the first-time viewer's view; knowing the intent fills the gaps the viewer will fall into. [15, 8 · P] [editor]
- **P-65 Tell the story back in one sentence from the script alone, then compare it to the spine,** because Coyne's foolscap and Seger's spine both start from a one-page or one-line statement of what the story is; the gap between the two is the diagnosis. [8, 15 · P] [editor]
- **P-66 Diagnose from the top down: story, then structure, then cause, then exposition, then lines,** because rewriting practice fixes big problems before small ones, and an upstream fault produces many downstream symptoms. [15, 8, 20 · P] [editor]
- **P-67 Fix the top one to three faults, not every fault,** because one rewrite round (MD-67) can't absorb a full overhaul, and fixing the cause usually removes its symptoms. [20 · P/I] [editor]
- **P-68 Repair by cutting, merging or reordering before adding,** because 02 §12 forbids patching structure with narration, and Clark cuts big first (02 §11). [02 §11–§12 · P] [editor]
- **P-69 Keep every line that already works, word for word,** because a doctor's job is often a narrow fix, and a full rewrite throws away tested material and invites new faults. [20 · P] [editor]
- **P-70 Re-run the tell-back after repair,** because a repair that breaks causality elsewhere has to be caught before the voice locks. [8 · I] [editor]

## 2. What a fantastic spine is

A spine is 5–7 plain sentences that are the plot, plus a short field sheet. It is written before any line.
"Plain" means no devices, no hooks, no attribution: subject, verb, object, joined by *but* or *therefore*.

### 2.1 The fields

| Field | What it holds | Test |
|---|---|---|
| **Story question** | "Will X get/stop/prove Y?" | Yes/no answerable; the climax answers it; MD-66 end point is stated. (P-2) |
| **Point of attack & end point** | The first and last moment of story time. | Nothing before is more than one line; nothing after is more than one line. (P-3, P-4) |
| **Protagonist + want** | One named person and a concrete, visible goal. | Viewer could tell when it is won or lost. (P-18) |
| **Stakes** | What the protagonist loses if they fail, in personal terms. | Picturable; stated before the danger peaks. (P-23, P-53) |
| **Opposition + its want** | One named person or force with its own motive. | Motive is on screen before it acts. (P-19, P-20) |
| **Cast** | ≤3 named people, each with an introduction moment (name, role, want). | Nobody acts before that moment. (P-37, P-39) |
| **Spine sentences (5–7)** | World+want → inciting incident → complications (one is the midpoint) → crisis → climax → resolution. | Every join is *but* or *therefore*; every sentence turns a value. (P-27, P-31) |
| **Information plan** | For each sentence: suspense, curiosity or surprise, and what the viewer knows that a character doesn't. | At least one stretch of suspense or irony. (P-47, P-49) |
| **Setups → payoffs** | Each planted detail and where it fires. | No orphan setup, no unprepared payoff. (P-60) |
| **Turn** | The reversal; which earlier sentence makes it inevitable. | Flips the expected answer, not the question. (P-50, P-51) |
| **Close question** | The question the resolution's answer creates. | Follows from the last line alone. (P-62, P-63) |
| **Out of scope** | Events deliberately left out or capped at one line. | Lists what the pitch tempted toward. (P-5) |

### 2.2 Dramatic, not merely causal

A causal spine says what happened because of what. A dramatic spine adds five things, and the editor
should check for each:
1. **An opponent with agency.** Someone wants the opposite and acts on it. Circumstance alone is weak. (P-19)
2. **A cost.** Each "but" takes something from the protagonist; each "therefore" spends something. (P-53)
3. **A dilemma.** At the crisis the protagonist must give something up to keep going. (P-11)
4. **A gap in knowledge.** The viewer knows or fears something the protagonist doesn't, or wants the cause of
   something already shown. (P-47, P-49)
5. **A reversal earned by a setup.** The turn was planted in sentence 1–3. (P-50, P-60)

A spine with all five and a closed question is the edge-of-seat version. Where the record lacks one
(often the dilemma), the spine says so instead of inventing it (P-55).

### 2.3 ILLUSTRATIVE worked example (non-AI; facts simplified, not checked to fact-sheet standard)

**ILLUSTRATIVE — topic spine (bad):** In 1854 cholera struck London. John Snow was a doctor. He made a map
of the deaths. The pump handle was removed. Years later his theory won. Today he is called the father
of epidemiology.
*Faults:* no want, no opposition, joins are "and then", the climax answers nothing, and the last two
sentences are aftermath.

**ILLUSTRATIVE — dramatic spine (good):**
- *Question:* Will Snow prove the water is killing Soho before the outbreak ends? *End point:* the handle comes off.
- *Protagonist + want:* John Snow, a doctor whose waterborne theory the establishment dismisses, wants proof.
- *Opposition:* the miasma consensus, embodied by a local curate, Henry Whitehead, who sets out to disprove him.
1. A doctor believes cholera spreads through water, but the medical world blames foul air.
2. But hundreds in one parish die within days, therefore he walks door to door listing the dead.
3. The deaths cluster around one street pump, but some who died lived nearer other pumps.
4. Therefore he traces each exception to someone who walked to that pump for its water. *(midpoint: he now has a case)*
5. But the parish board must act on a theory it rejects, and the outbreak is already fading. *(crisis for the board; for Snow, risk his standing on a claim the timing can't prove)*
6. Therefore he puts his map before them, and they order the pump handle removed. *(climax)*
7. The deaths stop, but no one can say whether the handle did it. *(resolution; close question: "Did the handle end it?")*
- *Information plan:* suspense from sentence 2 (the viewer knows the pump; the drinkers don't); surprise at sentence 3's exceptions.
- *Out of scope:* the curate's later conversion and the official report a year on: either one line or a different episode.

Note how the same record yields a second, equally valid episode with Whitehead as protagonist (he sets out to
disprove Snow and finds the case that proves him): choosing the protagonist chooses the story.

## 3. The story editor

### 3.1 Procedure
1. Read the script cold (lines only). (P-64)
2. Write the tell-back: one sentence of who wants what, what stood in the way, and how it came out. (P-65)
3. Read the spine and pitch; compare the tell-back to the spine's question and end point.
4. Run the diagnosis order below; stop collecting after the first three serious faults.
5. Choose one repair move per fault from §3.3; rewrite, keeping every line that works. (P-67–P-69)
6. Re-run the tell-back and the checks for steps 1–5 of §3.2 only. Ship, flagging anything left (MD-65).

### 3.2 Diagnosis order, ranked by importance

| # | Question | Why it ranks here |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Is it one story?** Can the tell-back be written as one question answered by one climax, inside the pitch's end point? | Wrong scope makes every later fix pointless; Rosewood v2 failed here first. (P-1–P-5) |
| 2 | **Whose story, wanting what?** Is the protagonist on screen in the first seconds with a visible want that drives the events? | Without a want, causes and conflict have nothing to attach to. (P-18, MD-29) |
| 3 | **Against whom, and why?** Is the opposition named, motivated before it acts, and are the stakes personal? | Conflict is the engine; an unmotivated opponent reads as random. (P-19–P-23) |
| 4 | **Does each beat cause the next?** No orphan effect; joins are but/therefore; each beat turns a value. | Causality is what turns facts into plot. (P-27–P-33) |
| 5 | **Can a stranger follow it?** Every person introduced (name, role, want) before acting; ≤3 named; no undecoded reference; no internal contradiction (timeline, who did what, naming). | Confusion kills a short faster than slowness. (P-37–P-41) |
| 6 | **Does it climb and turn?** Complications escalate; there is a midpoint or a crisis; the turn flips the expected answer and was planted. | This is the edge-of-seat layer; it only works on a sound chain. (P-10–P-15, P-47–P-53) |
| 7 | **Does it land?** The climax answers the question; resolution ≤2 lines; setups paid; close question follows from the last line. | The ending is judged against the question set in step 1. (P-59–P-63) |
| 8 | **Do the lines serve the plot?** Attribution, devices, words-per-rung, ear rules. | Line faults are real but cheap; fix only after 1–7. (02 §2–§3) |

**Contradictions check (inside step 5).** The editor lists every claim of time, sequence, role and
motive and checks each against the others and the fact sheet: a date that runs backward, a person
called ally then rival with no turn between, a motive stated two ways, a "first" that isn't. A
contradiction is a step-5 fault even when every fact is individually true.

### 3.3 Fault → repair moves

| Fault | Symptom in the script | Repair move (in order of preference) |
|---|---|---|
| Scope drift | Pitched episode becomes its aftermath; several outcomes | **Narrow scope:** restore the story question and end point; cut post-climax events to one line; move the point of attack later. (P-3, P-4) |
| Topic, not story | A list of facts about a subject | **Reframe around the protagonist's want:** pick the person whose want the record shows best; rewrite the first spine sentence as their want. (P-54) |
| Two protagonists | POV hops; no one's want dominates | **Choose one; demote the other to opposition** with their own want. (P-19, P-26) |
| Orphan event | An effect with no cause on screen (a suit with no grievance) | **Find the causal link** in the fact sheet and place it one beat earlier; if none exists, cut the event. (P-28) |
| "And then" chain | Beats in date order with no turns | **Reorder by consequence; collapse** beats that don't turn a value into one. (P-27, P-31) |
| Act before intro | A person questioned or acting before we know them | **Move the introduction** to their first appearance (name + role + want, face on screen); or **replace with a role**. (P-37, P-39) |
| Overcast | 4+ named people | **Cut or merge characters:** keep ≤3 names; others become roles or go. (P-39) |
| No opposition | Things happen to the protagonist; no one pushes back | **Personify the obstacle** with the documented person who embodied it; state their motive. (P-19, P-20) |
| Flat middle | Complications all the same size | **Escalate:** reorder from least to most costly; add the midpoint flip the record supports. (P-10, P-15) |
| Unearned turn | Reversal comes from nowhere | **Plant** its cause in the first third; or **change the point of attack** to start where the seed is visible. (P-50, P-60) |
| Turn changes the subject | Back half answers a different question | **Re-aim the turn** at the expected answer of the original question. (P-51) |
| No climax | Story trails off; question never answered | **Promote the decisive act** to the climax and end within two lines of it. (P-12, P-13) |
| Exposition dump | Context lines stop the train | **Convert to ammunition** (a fact someone uses); **move it just in time**; or cut. (P-34, P-35) |
| Close question off-thread | Question about a different conflict | **Rederive** from the last line: what question does that answer create? (P-62, P-63) |
| Device-as-structure | Beats named for techniques ("gold coin", "plant the bomb") carry single facts | **Rewrite rung purposes as plot functions** (which spine sentence, which value turns); keep a device only if it serves that function. (§4 C3) |
| Attribution clutter | "later testified", "one report says" on many lines | **Attribute once per source,** prefer an on-screen source label, and keep attribution before the claim. (§4 C5) |
| Internal contradiction | Timeline or role conflict within the script | **Correct to the fact sheet**; if both claims are sourced, pick the one the story needs and mark it disputed. (§3.2) |

## 4. Contradictions and overlaps with existing rules

| # | Rule A | Rule B / finding | Recommended resolution |

**Rules that likely caused the bad script (summary).** C1 and C2 together licensed a turn and a kicker
that left the episode; C3 and C4 replaced plot functions with devices and per-rung novelty; C5 added
clauses with no story; and nothing required introducing a person before they act (C6, C9, C10) or
deriving the close question from the landing (C8). Each was a reasonable rule read alone; none of them
was tied to a story question.

## 5. Gaps this pass fills

1. **No scope rule.** Nothing defined where a story starts and stops; the dramatic question now sets both (P-1–P-6).
2. **No opposition.** Existing craft had want and "complication" but no opponent with a motive (P-19–P-20, P-26).
3. **No crisis or climax definition.** The four buckets had a turn and a landing but no dilemma and no answer to the question (P-11, P-12).
4. **No value-change test.** "Something new" was measured in facts; a beat now must turn a value (P-31).
5. **No character-introduction protocol** or name count (P-37–P-41).
6. **No subplot guidance** (P-42–P-46).
7. **No information-state planning** beyond Hitchcock: suspense, curiosity and surprise as separate tools (P-47).
8. **No setup/payoff ledger** (P-60).
9. **No derivation rule for the close question** (P-62, P-63).
10. **No spine artifact** with testable fields (§2).
11. **No story-editing method:** cold read, tell-back, ranked diagnosis, repair moves (§3).
12. **Story vs backstory vs aftermath** was implicit in "the train" but never operational (P-4).
13. **Internal contradiction check** was absent; facts were checked one by one, never against each other (§3.2).

**Still thin.** No controlled evidence on spine-first writing for short video; the timing windows (P-16) are
inference. Creator practice (MagnatesMedia, Johnny Harris, Vox) on plotting remains secondhand. Several book
principles were confirmed only through secondary summaries this session (Egri, Seger, Franklin, Story Grid).
