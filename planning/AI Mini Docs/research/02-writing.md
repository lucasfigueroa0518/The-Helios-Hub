# 02 · The Writing: the writer's brain

Desk research for 60-second vertical mini-documentaries. It covers narration and on-screen words, and how the writer hands off to the director. Compiled 2026-10-28.

**How to read a line.** `- **Do X** — because Y. [source · grade] (roles: …)`

- **Grades:** **E** = evidence (a peer-reviewed study or a platform's own data). **P** = professional practice from an established craft authority or newsroom training. **F** = creator folklore (untested creator advice or a leaked document). **I** = my inference, built from the cited sources.
- **Roles:** **writer** (narration and on-screen words), **director** (visual telling), **voice** (delivery of the cloned narration), **checks** (automated or human review).
- **Altitude:** every principle is written to fit any story. Illustrative examples come only from the cited source and are marked *(source example)*. No examples come from AI news.
- **Quotations:** craft text is paraphrased throughout. Apart from short concept names (gold coins, status details, point of attack), the one verbatim quote is marked.

## Core of the craft (if you read nothing else)

1. A 60-second piece carries one story, told as a person meeting a complication, and it moves by "but" and "therefore".
2. The narration says only what the pictures and sound can't. It leaves room for silence, and the picture often replaces a line.
3. Write for the ear: one thought per sentence, the subject next to its verb, the strongest word last, and attribution before the claim.
4. Curiosity is a felt gap the viewer can almost close. Open it in the first seconds, and answer each question while opening the next.
5. Suspense comes from telling the viewer what is coming, not from hiding it. Save surprise for the turn.
6. Lock the story as audio first. The writer hands over lines, on-screen words, and the intent of each beat. The director decides how the beat is seen.

## 1. Story first: what the words are for

- **Find the one-sentence story before writing a line, and make one main point** — because a one-minute piece has room for only one big idea; if you can't tell it to a friend in a sentence, keep cutting. [4, 3 · P] (roles: writer | checks)
- **Frame the story as a sympathetic character meeting a complication they confront and resolve** — because Franklin and Hart both define story this way; a topic without a struggle is a report, not a story. [22, 20 · P] (roles: writer)
- **Choose a lead figure the viewer can root for, flaws and all; avoid making a pure victim or pure villain the protagonist** — because Hart finds that victims and perpetrators make weak protagonists. [20 · P] (roles: writer)
- **Keep the cast to one or two central figures** — because Hart warns that frequent point-of-view shifts leave readers lost, and caps even long narratives at three or four developed characters. [20 · P/I] (roles: writer | director)
- **When the material is more explanation than action, tell it as an explanatory narrative: one line of action with short digressions for context** — because Hart says forcing explanatory material into a full dramatic arc fails. [20 · P] (roles: writer)
- **Arrange the facts; never invent them** — because Bernard holds that documentary story comes from creatively arranging real material, so drama is built from selection and order; her working shape is a compelling beginning, an unexpected middle and a satisfying end. [15 · P] (roles: writer | checks)
- **Build a single narrative "train" that carries everything else** — because Bernard's train, the main line of action, is what keeps viewers watching; context and theme ride on it rather than stopping it. [15, 16 · P] (roles: writer | director)
- **Choose the point of attack on purpose** — because Bernard calls the entry point one of the hardest decisions; it brings the viewer into the world of the story and sets what they will wait for. [15 · P] (roles: writer | director)
- **Give meaning one clear line** — because Glass pairs every anecdote with a moment of reflection that answers why we are watching, and the CSIC gloss on Bernard warns that meaning is the passenger most often left off the train. [27, 16 · P] (roles: writer)

## 2. Writing for the ear

- **Write the way you talk** — because Block makes it rule 1 of broadcast writing, and NPR's mantra is (verbatim) "If you wouldn't say it that way, don't write it that way." [1, 2 · P] (roles: writer | voice)
- **Limit each sentence to one thought** — because listeners can't reread, and NPR teaches that the ear handles one fact or idea per sentence. [1, 2 · P] (roles: writer | checks)
- **Keep subjects next to their verbs and avoid dependent clauses** — because spoken syntax is simple; NPR shows a clause-laden sentence split into two short ones *(source example: a sentence about a father who taught physics)*. [2, 23 · P] (roles: writer)
- **Default to short sentences, with about 16 words as a ceiling** — because BBC correspondent Allan Little treats anything longer as too long when scripting to pictures. [6 · P] (roles: writer | checks)
- **Vary sentence length on purpose to set the pace** — because Clark treats sentence length as the main control on speed, and Kallaway mixes short, medium and long lines so delivery doesn't flatten. [23, 36 · P/F] (roles: writer | voice)
- **Put the word you want stressed at the end of the sentence** — because Block and Clark both place emphatic words at the close; begin with what the listener knows and end with what is new. [1, 23 · P] (roles: writer | voice)
- **Land the climactic idea in the shortest sentence of the piece** — because Clark advises giving your best thought the shortest sentence. [23 · P] (roles: writer | voice)
- **Use active verbs and familiar words; don't build the main clause on a form of "to be"** — because Block calls these dead starts, with one exception: a short, dramatic statement *(source example: the big bad wolf is dead)*. Joyce adds that verbs and pictures in the listener's mind beat stacked adjectives. [1, 3, 23 · P] (roles: writer)
- **Allow fragments and contractions the way people speak** — because conversation uses them; Block adds that contractions which could be misheard (can/can't) need care. [3, 1 · P] (roles: writer | voice)
- **Signpost every change of direction out loud** — because audio has no headings or white space, so a line that says "we're moving on" replaces the visual cues of print. [2, 3, 4 · P] (roles: writer)
- **Repeat the key noun instead of swapping in synonyms** — because listeners forget names and titles quickly, so elegant variation reads as a new subject to the ear. [2 · I] (roles: writer)
- **Read every draft aloud and rewrite whatever trips the tongue** — because a line that stumbles is print writing; Block, Joyce and MacAdam all make reading aloud the core edit. [1, 3, 2 · P] (roles: writer | voice | checks)
- **Mark the script for pace, pauses and emphasis before recording** — because NPR training recommends marking scripts to sound natural. [5 · P] (roles: voice)

## 3. Facts out loud: attribution, numbers, names

- **Put attribution before the assertion** — because Block's rule and NPR's guidance agree that speakers never hang attribution after the claim, and a listener needs the source before weighing the claim. [1, 2 · P] (roles: writer)
- **Attribute where a claim is contestable, not on every sentence** — because Block notes that once the source is clear, not every following sentence needs it, and authoritative facts can stand bare. [1 · P] (roles: writer | checks)
- **Use few numbers, rounded, and never make the viewer do arithmetic** — because spoken numbers are misheard and forgotten, and comparing two figures pulls the listener out of the story. [1, 2 · P] (roles: writer | director)
- **Hand exact figures, full names, titles and places to the screen** — because NPR's short-video team uses a lower third rather than reading a name and title, and a map rather than describing a location. [4 · P] (roles: writer | director)
- **Don't open on an unfamiliar name; say who the person is or what they did first** — because Block finds that an unknown name at the start means nothing to a listener. [1 · P] (roles: writer)
- **Don't label the news as shocking, amazing or tragic; let the facts do it** — because Block warns against characterizing news, and an adjective claim invites disbelief where a fact earns the feeling. [1 · P] (roles: writer | checks)
- **When narration names a person, give the director a beat to show their face clearly** — because Boyd Huppert argues you shouldn't introduce a character without looking them in the eye. [7 · P] (roles: writer | director)

## 4. Writing to picture: restraint and silence

- **Never narrate what the viewer can already see; add context, meaning or consequence** — because broadcast training says words should add what pictures can't *(source example: rather than "this is the village church," give a fact about it)*. [7, 9 · P] (roles: writer | checks)
- **Give the narration only what is impossible to convey in pictures and sound** — because that is BBC wildlife cameraman John Aitchison's test for narration. [14 · P] (roles: writer)
- **Once picture exists, first look for what to cut** — because Attenborough's first step after writing a commentary is to see what he can remove, and he warns against being too wordy. [13 · P] (roles: writer | checks)
- **Let images replace lines, and write no more narration than the pictures can carry** — because Ward finds that much of writing for film is removing things, since images can do the narration's work, and TV practice is to write tight to the footage you have. [10, 7 · P] (roles: writer | director)
- **Leave deliberate gaps with no narration** — because the BBC's TV training says pictures need room to breathe and sound matters; less is nearly always more when scripting to pictures. [6 · P] (roles: writer | director | voice)
- **Write in scenes you can see** — because Ward drafts by picturing each scene, and Clark asks writers to work from varied camera angles and to treat the notebook as a camera. [11, 23 · P] (roles: writer | director)
- **Bring a sound or image in first, then name it** — because a brief "what's that?" creates curiosity that the next line resolves *(source example: walrus sounds rise before the narrator explains the beach)*. [3 · P] (roles: writer | director)
- **Use natural sound and silence as the throttle on pace** — because Huppert treats natural sound as the control for speeding up or slowing down a story, and writes fewer words to make room for it. [8 · P] (roles: director | voice)
- **Use narration to complement the image, to contradict it, or to pose a question a later image answers** — because Rossi lists these as the legitimate jobs of voice-over, and contradiction creates tension. [17 · P] (roles: writer | director)
- **Carry key facts in on-screen words so the piece survives muted viewing** — because Facebook's feed research advises video that is understandable regardless of sound, and NPR moves names and places to the screen. [39, 4 · E/P] (roles: writer | director)

## 5. Voice, point of view and tense

- **Choose one narrator stance and hold it for the whole piece** — because shifting point of view confuses the audience. [20 · P] (roles: writer | voice)
- **At key moments, tell the scene through one participant's experience** — because Wolfe's third device presents each scene through a particular character's eyes, which puts the viewer inside the event. [19 · P] (roles: writer)
- **Sound like a person, not an institution** — because NPR's short-video team wants a video to sound human-made, and MacAdam warns that non-conversational phrasing turns you into a sterile news reader. [4, 2 · P] (roles: writer | voice)
- **Write for the specific voice that will read it** — because NPR holds there is no single correct radio voice; if a phrase comes naturally to that speaker, it works. [2 · P] (roles: writer | voice)
- **Use the present tense for immediacy where it fits, and keep tense steady within a scene** — because Block recommends present-tense verbs where appropriate, and a tense change mid-scene breaks the illusion. [1 · P/I] (roles: writer | voice)
- **Have the narrator understand each line, then get out of its way** — because Ward says a narrator must know what the lines really mean and then not perform over them. [11 · P] (roles: voice)
- **Address the viewer directly, but rarely** — because Joyce allows direct address ("Now, …") sparingly, and constant "you" wears thin. [3 · P] (roles: writer)

## 6. Scenes, detail and the words of others

- **Build the piece from scenes, not summary** — because Wolfe's first device is scene-by-scene construction, and Hart separates stories, which convey experience through scenes, from reports, which are abstract. [19, 20 · P] (roles: writer | director)
- **Move up and down the ladder of abstraction: concrete detail, a line of meaning, then concrete again** — because Clark and Hart both teach that meaning comes from the general, but belief comes from the specific. [23, 20 · P] (roles: writer)
- **Use status details: the objects, clothes, manners and settings that show who someone is and where they stand** — because Wolfe ranked status detail among his four core devices and the least understood. [19 · P] (roles: writer | director)
- **Get the specific name, number or object, and build character from a mosaic of such details** — because Clark's "name of the dog" tool shows that one exact detail makes a scene real, and his final tool builds character from accumulated particulars rather than description. [23 · P] (roles: writer)
- **Choose the source material that carries emotion or decision over material that carries information** — because Joyce finds that tape conveying emotion is stronger than tape that merely explains, and Wolfe used real dialogue to establish character. [3, 19 · P] (roles: writer | director)
- **Make each quote, document or clip carry a single point** — because Joyce gives each actuality one job; two points means two moments. [3 · P] (roles: writer | director)
- **Don't say in narration what the next quote or document is about to say** — because Joyce calls that stepping on the act; it spends the reveal twice. [3 · P] (roles: writer | checks)
- **Let the source finish the narrator's sentence** — because Joyce looks for material that continues the narration's thought ("and…", "so…", "that's because…"), which makes copy and evidence feel like one voice. [3 · P] (roles: writer | director)
- **Pair every action with a reaction** — because Huppert finds an action only half as strong without the response to it. [8 · P] (roles: writer | director)

## 7. Structure and causality

- **Join beats with "but" or "therefore," never "and then"** — because Parker and Stone teach that causal links create story, while a chain of "and thens" is a list. [33 · P] (roles: writer | checks)
- **Let action drive momentum, with brief reflection between actions** — because Glass describes the anecdote as a sequence of actions that pulls forward on its own, punctuated by moments that say what it means. [27 · P] (roles: writer)
- **Raise a question at the start and promise its answer by the end** — because NPR teaches that the best audio stories, even daily news, run on a question set up front. [2 · P] (roles: writer)
- **Reveal the details bit by bit** — because Joyce's campfire model teases a great or terrible revelation at the start and pays it out gradually to hold attention to the end. [3, 2 · P] (roles: writer)
- **Place one hinge point early, the line where the story steps back and says why it matters, and spread the rest of the context through the piece** — because Joyce names this pivot as the moment that tells the audience what the story is about, and NPR disperses nut-graf ideas where the narrative needs them. [3, 2 · P] (roles: writer)
- **Escalate: each beat raises the stakes, with the biggest moment held for last** — because the leaked MrBeast guide describes this "stair stepping" as what keeps viewers watching to the resolution. [34 · F] (roles: writer | director)
- **Place rewards through the middle, not only in the opening** — because Clark's gold coins, small payoffs spaced along the path, keep the audience from drifting out mid-story. [23 · P] (roles: writer | checks)
- **Know the last line before writing the first** — because Scanlan advises writing the ending first to plan the route, and Hoyos reportedly writes the hook and last line first. [21, 35 · P/F] (roles: writer)
- **Keep the structure invisible** — because McPhee wants structure to show no more than a person's bones, with the shaping unnoticed. [24 · P] (roles: writer)
- **Build the structure fresh for each story instead of filling a template** — because McPhee rebuilds structure for every piece, and NPR finds templates go stale quickly. [24, 2 · P] (roles: writer)
- **Vary the length of narration runs and source moments** — because equal-length blocks make a piece sound machine-made. [3 · P] (roles: writer | director)

## 8. Curiosity and suspense mechanics

- **Open a gap the viewer feels close to closing** — because Loewenstein defines curiosity as attention fixed on a gap in knowledge; it needs some prior knowledge, and it peaks at moderate confidence. [28, 29 · E] (roles: writer)
- **Prime curiosity with a specific, incomplete fragment** — because Loewenstein's account holds that a little intriguing information starts the itch, while total ignorance produces none. [28 · E] (roles: writer)
- **Invite the viewer to guess before the reveal** — because Brod and Breitwieser found that generating a prediction raises curiosity, and pupil data suggest a bigger surprise at the answer. [30 · E] (roles: writer | director)
- **Make sure a felt question comes before every key fact** — because Kang et al. found curiosity engages reward circuitry and improves later recall of surprising answers. [29 · E] (roles: writer)
- **Prefer suspense to surprise: show the bomb under the table, and let the viewer know what a participant doesn't** — because Hitchcock's parable shows that telling the audience what's coming turns seconds of surprise into minutes of suspense; dramatic irony works the same way. [32 · P] (roles: writer | director)
- **Foreshadow the turn** — because Clark lists foreshadowing as the writer's tool for generating suspense. [23 · P] (roles: writer)
- **Keep raising and answering questions** — because Glass teaches that a constant cycle of question and answer, the bait, holds an audience, and Joyce aims for the listener asking "what happened next?" and then answers. [27, 3 · P] (roles: writer)
- **Answer every question the piece raises, or hand it off explicitly as the cliffhanger** — because Block warns against raising questions you don't answer, and a forgotten loop feels like a cheat, not suspense. [1 · P/I] (roles: writer | checks)
- **Treat open loops as a pull toward resolution, not a memory trick** — because a 2025 meta-analysis found no reliable memory advantage for unfinished tasks (Zeigarnik), but a general urge to resume them (Ovsiankina). [31 · E] (roles: writer | checks)

## 9. Hooks, re-hooks and payoff in short form

- **Win the first ~3 seconds with the opening line and image working together** — because viewers decide fast. TikTok's ad data show most top-CTR videos put the key message in the first 3 seconds; Facebook found recall after a quarter-second of feed exposure and 1.7 seconds average dwell on mobile. [4, 38, 39 · E/P] (roles: writer | director)
- **Open on a bold statement, surprise, question or mystery, and cut the background** — because NPR's short-video producers found you need to explain far less up top than you think. [4 · P] (roles: writer)
- **Make the hook a specific promise, then keep it** — because the MrBeast guide treats a mismatch between promise and content as a bait-and-switch that drives viewers off, and Hoyos ends right after delivering the hook's promise. [34, 35 · F] (roles: writer | checks)
- **Make the hook legible on mute: the on-screen words alone must carry the question** — because feed video is often watched without sound. [39, 35 · E/F] (roles: writer | director)
- **Put interesting material early instead of saving it** — because the leaked MrBeast guide calls the first minute the most important and says to put strong content up front. [34 · F] (roles: writer)
- **Re-hook at each beat turn with a new question, a reversal or a raised stake** — because Kallaway describes a short as a hook, then a "dance" of context and conflict with re-hooks, then a closing hook; the MrBeast guide schedules re-engagement points. [36, 34 · F] (roles: writer)
- **Flag what matters with a spoken signpost and a change of pace** — because NPR's short-video team uses signpost phrases and pace changes to tell viewers where to pay attention. [4 · P] (roles: writer | voice)

## 10. Endings

- **End right after the payoff lands, without signalling the wrap-up** — because the MrBeast guide calls for abrupt endings to protect retention and warns against flagging the end early, and Hoyos ends the moment the promise is delivered. [34, 35 · F] (roles: writer | director)
- **For a cliffhanger, resolve this piece's question, then open a concrete next question about what happens next** — because Block's rule against unanswered questions and the campfire "what happened next?" pull are compatible only if the episode closes its own loop first. [1, 3 · I] (roles: writer)
- **Consider a loop ending, where the last line flows back into the first** — because Hoyos reports retention above 100% from viewers who rewatch looping shorts. [35 · F] (roles: writer | director)

## 11. Word economy at 60 seconds

- **Budget about 110–140 narrated words for 60 seconds** — because conversational English runs about 150 words a minute, and dramatic narration needs pauses, silent picture beats and a first-second visual hook. [40, 6, 13 · P/I] (roles: writer | voice | checks)
- **Plan about five to seven beats (hook, setup, complication, escalation, turn, payoff or cliffhanger)** — because at roughly 8–12 seconds per beat that is all 60 seconds holds; no study fixes the number. [I] (roles: writer | director)
- **List the elements the story can't live without, narrow the list, then put it in order** — because that is NPR's method for building a one-minute video. [4 · P] (roles: writer | checks)
- **Cut big first, then small** — because Clark removes whole sections before trimming words, and Block's rule is few names, few numbers, few facts, few words. [23, 1 · P] (roles: writer | checks)
- **Expect many drafts** — because Kurzgesagt reports about a dozen script drafts to cut away the unnecessary. [18 · P] (roles: writer)

## 12. The writer–director handoff

- **Lock the story as audio first (a radio cut or paper edit), then build picture to it** — because editors test narrative on paper and in audio before images, and Kurzgesagt's voice-over sets the timing for its animators. [41, 18 · P] (roles: writer | director)
- **Hand the director, per beat: the narration lines, the on-screen words, the beat's intent (what the viewer should feel, learn or wonder) and the evidence available** — because Harris reportedly pairs every scripted line with visual notes, and in the Burns model the writer owns the words while others own interviews and music. [42, 11 · F/P/I] (roles: writer | director)
- **Write to the material that exists** — because Joyce warns against forcing gathered evidence to justify a story written in advance; let the material steer. [3 · P] (roles: writer | director)
- **Expect lines to be cut or rewritten once picture exists** — because the Burns team kept rewriting through the edit, and Ward finds images replacing narration. [12, 10 · P] (roles: writer | director)
- **Fix weak structure in the story, not by adding narration** — because Rossi treats voice-over used as a patch for structure as a flaw in any school of filmmaking. [17 · P] (roles: writer | director | checks)
- **Leave shots, motion, transitions and music to the director; the writer may flag only a must-see item (a face, a document, a place) when a line depends on it** — because in the Burns model music and assembly decisions come later from the director and editors, not the writer. [11 · P/I] (roles: writer | director)

## Anti-patterns (don't)

- **Don't describe what is on screen** — because it wastes the scarcest resource (words) and insults the picture. [7, 9, 14 · P] (roles: writer | checks)
- **Don't open with "There is/It is," a quotation, a pronoun, an unfamiliar name or "as expected"** — because Block lists these as dead or confusing starts for the ear. [1 · P] (roles: writer | checks)
- **Don't hang attribution after the claim** — because people never speak that way, and the listener judges the claim before knowing its source. [1, 2 · P] (roles: writer | checks)
- **Don't stack numbers or make the viewer compare figures in their head** — because spoken numbers get lost, and arithmetic pulls attention from the story. [2 · P] (roles: writer | checks)
- **Don't link beats with "and then"** — because it yields a sequence, not a story. [33 · P] (roles: writer | checks)
- **Don't front-load background or the five Ws** — because it delays the hook and removes the reason to stay. [4, 2 · P] (roles: writer)
- **Don't step on the source by previewing its words** — because the evidence moment then repeats the narration and loses its punch. [3 · P] (roles: writer | checks)
- **Don't announce what's coming ("in this video…," "you won't believe…")** — because it spends the surprise; let the picture and sound deliver it. [7 · P] (roles: writer)
- **Don't characterize ("shocking," "incredible," "tragic")** — because labels replace evidence and invite doubt. [1 · P] (roles: writer | checks)
- **Don't bait-and-switch: never hook with a promise the piece doesn't pay** — because a broken promise drives viewers off and costs trust. [34 · F] (roles: writer | checks)
- **Don't raise a question and abandon it** — because unanswered questions read as sloppiness, not mystery. [1 · P] (roles: writer | checks)
- **Don't ping-pong between many voices or sources** — because listeners lose track of who is who. [3 · P] (roles: writer | director)
- **Don't make the same point twice** — because redundancy kills momentum in a format with no spare seconds. [3 · P] (roles: writer | checks)
- **Don't write print syntax (long dependent clauses, inverted pyramid)** — because the ear can't parse it in one pass. [2 · P] (roles: writer | checks)
- **Don't fill every second with words** — because pictures and sound need room, and silence sets up the key line. [6, 13 · P] (roles: writer | voice)
- **Don't embellish, composite or invent detail to heighten drama** — because documentary story is arrangement of real material, and a single invented detail discredits the rest. [15 · P] (roles: writer | checks)
- **Don't use narration to patch a weak story** — because it fixes nothing and makes the piece sound like a lecture. [17 · P] (roles: writer | director)
- **Don't sound like a press release or a news reader** — because institutional phrasing breaks the intimacy that keeps people watching. [4, 2 · P] (roles: writer | voice)
- **Don't run every story through the same template** — because templates go stale fast and the audience learns the pattern. [2, 24 · P] (roles: writer)

## Hook and re-hook patterns (abstract catalogue)

**Opening hooks** (first ~3 seconds; line and image together, readable on mute)
1. **In the middle of the action.** Open at the highest-tension moment, then rewind to how it got there. [3, 15]
2. **Stakes statement.** One plain declarative line that states a consequence for someone. [4]
3. **Mystery artifact.** Show a document, object or place first; the line asks what it means. [3, 4]
4. **Contradiction.** The image and narration disagree, or an expected outcome is flipped. [17]
5. **Bomb under the table.** Tell the viewer what is coming that the people in the story don't know. [32]
6. **The end first.** State the outcome, so the question becomes how and why. [I; see "Where the field disagrees"]
7. **Prediction prompt.** Ask the viewer to bet on what happens. [30]
8. **Direct question.** Use sparingly; one school allows it and another bans it. [4, 1]

**Re-hooks** (at each beat turn, roughly every 8–12 seconds in a 60-second piece; the interval is inference)
- **"But" reversal.** The beat ends with a turn that undoes the expectation it set up. [33]
- **Answer-and-reopen.** Close the current question and open a sharper one in the same line. [27, 3]
- **Raised stakes.** The next beat makes the consequence bigger or more personal. [34]
- **Sound or image first.** Let a new element land unexplained for a second, then name it. [3]
- **Planted detail pays off.** Something shown early returns with new meaning (a gold coin). [23]
- **Spoken signpost.** A short line that tells the viewer this is the part that matters. [4]
- **Silence before the key line.** A beat with no narration, so the next sentence lands. [6, 8 · I]

**Closers**
- **Abrupt payoff.** End on the reveal; no summary and no sign-off. [34, 35]
- **Next-question cliffhanger.** Close this story's question, then open one about what happens next. [1, 3 · I]
- **Loop.** The last line hands off to the first. [35]
- **Reflection line.** One sentence of meaning, then out. [27]

## Where the field disagrees

- **Eyes-closed test or picture-dependence?** A BBC school resource says a good TV script should make sense with the eyes closed [45]. Aitchison, Attenborough and Ward want narration to carry only what the picture can't, so it should not stand alone [14, 13, 10]. *For this format:* narration plus on-screen words should make sense on audio alone, and picture plus on-screen words should make sense on mute.
- **Opening with a question.** Block advises against it, since it sounds like a quiz show or a commercial [1]. NPR's short-video team lists a question or mystery as a valid opener [4]. *Usable middle ground:* a question implied by an image or a statement usually beats a literal spoken question.
- **Closing every loop, or leaving people hanging.** Block warns against unanswered questions [1]. Campfire and serial practice keeps viewers wanting more [3, 34]. The reconciling rule (close the episode's question, open the next) is my inference.
- **Spoilers and "the end first."** Leavitt and Christenfeld (2011) reported that spoiled stories were enjoyed more; Johnson and Rosenbaum (2015) reported the opposite on several measures [43, 44, both unverified this session]. Hitchcock's suspense logic favors telling the viewer what's coming [32]. No source settles it for short documentary.
- **Script first, or narration last?** In the Burns model the writer drafts the script before the cut [11]. Many editors cut to the evidence first and write narration last, to fit the edit [41, 3]. For an AI pipeline built on real photos and documents, writing to the available material comes close to both.
- **Front-load or escalate?** The MrBeast guide says to put strong material early *and* to stair-step to the biggest moment last [34]. Clark's gold coins split the difference [23]. The tension is real when material is thin.
- **Should a documentary use narration at all?** Many documentary filmmakers distrust authoritative "voice of God" narration, and Rossi frames the debate [17]. Here narration is a given, so the lesson that transfers is restraint.
- **Present tense or past?** Block recommends the present where appropriate [1]. I found no source showing which tense grips more in short-form documentary (gap).
- **Do open loops "stick" in memory?** Creator advice often cites the Zeigarnik effect. A 2025 meta-analysis found the memory effect unreliable, while the tendency to resume interrupted tasks holds [31].

## 13. Writing for the muted viewer (added 2026-10-28)

**What the numbers say.** The "80–85% watch with the sound off" figure is old and comes from the Facebook feed. It does not describe today's short-form players. Every piece still has to work on mute.

- **Facebook feed, 2016:** Digiday's "85%" (May 2016) is not a Facebook measurement. It is self-reported publisher and agency data: LittleThings about 85%, Mic 85% of its 30-second views, MEC 85–90% for branded video, and PopSugar 50–80%. At the time, the feed autoplayed muted and counted a view at 3 seconds [46]. In February 2017, Facebook began fading feed video sound in unless the phone was on silent [47]. Facebook's own 2016 data said 41% of video ads lost their meaning without sound, and that captions raised view time by 12% on average [54].
- **Cross-platform survey, 2019:** Verizon Media and Publicis Media surveyed 5,616 US adults aged 18–54 in April 2019. 69% had watched video with the sound off in public, and 25% had done so in private. Coverage also reports that 80% were more likely to finish a video when captions were available [48]. The "92% sound-off on mobile" and "83%" figures appear in one write-up but not in the other (unverified).
- **Instagram Reels:** Meta said in November 2024 that over 75% of Instagram Reels views are sound-on [49]. A Meta summary from December 2025 says over 80% of daily Reels plays are sound-on, reported secondhand by PPC Land [50]. So roughly 20–25% of Reels plays are silent.
- **TikTok:** TikTok has not published a sound-on share. Its own Kantar research (fieldwork January 2021, published June 2021) found that 88% of users said sound is essential to TikTok, and 73% said they would stop and look at ads with audio. Both figures are self-reported and commissioned by the platform [51].
- **YouTube Shorts:** I found no published sound-on share. Google's current Shorts ads guidance recommends sound-on assets and says sound raises conversions by over 20% (underlying study not shown) [52]. The often-quoted "95% of YouTube ads are audible" comes from August 2016 internal data and covers YouTube ads generally, not Shorts. It counts an ad as audible if the volume reached 10% at any point [53].
- **Folklore:** "80% of social video is watched on mute" circulates on vendor blogs without a primary source (unverified). For these three platforms, the best evidence says most plays are sound-on. Muted viewers remain a large minority, though, including viewers in public and deaf and hard-of-hearing viewers. The craft below assumes both audiences on every play.

**Principles**

*Captions as the primary text track*

- **Burn in captions on every piece, word for word with the narration** — because roughly a fifth of Reels plays are silent, and BBC social assets tend to carry burnt-in subtitles. A review of 100+ studies also links same-language captions to better comprehension, attention and memory (a critic notes many of those studies involve second-language or special-needs viewers). [49, 50, 64, 60 · E/P] (roles: writer | director | checks)
- **Keep narration at or under the caption reading rate of about 160–180 words per minute, and slow down for dense lines** — because that is the BBC's general-audience reading rate. Netflix caps adult subtitles at 20 characters per second, and DCMP caps upper-level educational media at 160 wpm. At the ~150 wpm narration pace in §11, verbatim captions fit, so the risk is short bursts of fast speech. [55, 56, 57 · P] (roles: writer | voice | checks)
- **When a caption is too fast to read, fix the script, not the caption** — because broadcast captioners condense speech to protect reading time, but in a narrated short a caption that differs from the voice reads as an error. The BBC prefers verbatim and spreads any edit evenly across a sentence. [55, 57 · I] (roles: writer | checks)
- **Hold each caption for at least about 0.3 seconds per word, and longer for names, figures and unfamiliar terms, but don't pad it below speech pace** — because those are the BBC's display-time rules. In an eye-tracking study, viewers kept up with subtitles at 20 characters per second, while slow subtitles caused more re-reading and less enjoyment. [55, 58 · E/P] (roles: director | checks)
- **Break captions at phrase boundaries; never split an article from its noun, a first name from a last name, or a verb from its auxiliary** — because line breaks that ignored syntax raised cognitive load in an eye-tracking study, and the BBC and Netflix both codify these breaks. [59, 55, 56 · E/P] (roles: writer | director | checks)
- **Show captions as phrase blocks; if you highlight the spoken word, do it inside a phrase that stays on screen, not one word at a time** — because no peer-reviewed study supports word-by-word captions (the completion-lift claims come from vendors), and the evidence that does exist favours whole syntactic chunks. [59 · I] (roles: director | checks)
- **On 9:16, set captions at up to ~90% of the frame width and up to three lines, high in the lower third, clear of faces, documents and the app's interface** — because those are the BBC's vertical-video rules. Meta also tells advertisers to keep key messages inside the Reels safe zone. [55, 49 · P] (roles: director | checks)
- **Change captions on the cut, and never let one hang across a shot change** — because the BBC aligns subtitles to shot changes and splits or delays them to avoid straddling a cut. The writer's line lengths should fit the shot lengths. [55 · P] (roles: writer | director | checks)
- **Emphasise at most one word per caption, by colour or weight, and make it the word the sentence lands on** — because the BBC uses stress capitals sparingly, AJ+ uses colour on key words only, and §2 already puts the strongest word last. [55, 63 · P/I] (roles: writer | director)

*On-screen text beyond captions*

- **Put names, roles, places, dates and sources in short labels next to what they name, not in sentences** — because Mayer's redundancy research allows extra on-screen text only when it is short, keyword-like and placed beside the graphic it describes. NPR's short-video team moves names and places to the screen. [61, 4 · E/P] (roles: writer | director)
- **Show each key number as one large figure paired with an image, one number per screen** — because AJ+ presents statistics as big, simple figures paired with an image, and spoken numbers get lost (§3). [63, 2 · P] (roles: writer | director)
- **When a quote or document carries the turn, show it verbatim on screen. The narrator names the speaker and stays quiet while the words show** — because DCMP requires quotes to be verbatim, and Netflix removes on-screen-text subtitles that repeat what is already on screen and never mixes them with dialogue. If narration read the quote aloud, the caption and the card would show the same words twice. [57, 56 · P/I] (roles: writer | director)
- **Limit each frame to captions plus one other text element. No logo during captions** — because in the Verizon/Publicis test, pairing captions with a brand logo cut ad memory by 12%. When on-screen text overlaps speech, Netflix keeps only the most story-relevant message. [48, 56 · E/P] (roles: director | checks)
- **Spell names and technical terms exactly as the source spells them, and give a new term extra screen time** — because the BBC allows more display time for unfamiliar words, and Clark and Mayer find printed text helps when narration contains technical or unfamiliar words. [55, 61 · E/P] (roles: writer | checks)

*A hook that reads on mute*

- **Put the hook's words on screen from frame one, before the first spoken word, in large text over a striking image** — because AJ+ replaced its logo opener with a bold image and a posed question in big text. Facebook found recall after a quarter-second in feed, and NowThis treats the first frame as the teaser. §9 asks for a hook legible on mute; this sets its timing. [63, 39, 62 · E/P] (roles: writer | director)
- **Make the on-screen hook and the spoken hook one promise, with the screen using fewer words** — because a sound-on viewer takes in both, and two different hooks split attention. A Meta-commissioned study of 100 Reels ads found that delivering the message in both audio and visuals raised the odds of top-20% brand interest 1.8×, and audio-visual hooks in the first 1–2 seconds raised purchase-intent odds 1.5×. [50 · E] (roles: writer | director)

*Making the picture carry the story*

- **Run a mute pass on every cut: picture, captions and labels alone must carry each "but" and "therefore"** — because Facebook found 41% of video ads meaningless without sound, and Vox built an explainer with only animation and overlaid images, so it could be followed silently. [54, 62 · E/P] (roles: checks | director)
- **Give every beat one visible change that shows the turn: a new document, a map pin, a before-and-after** — because a muted viewer gets the causal chain only from the picture. NowThis built stories as big text over short clips. [62 · I] (roles: director | writer)
- **Give every sound-driven payoff a visual twin** — because a reveal carried by a music drop, a sound effect or the narrator's tone does not exist on mute. [I] (roles: writer | director | checks)

*Dividing the work between voice and text*

- **Split the jobs: narration carries cause, meaning and feeling; labels carry who, what, when and how much; the picture carries the evidence** — because narrated graphics teach better than narration plus duplicate printed text, with exceptions for short keywords. It extends §4: words add what the picture can't. [61, 7, 14 · E/P/I] (roles: writer | director)
- **When the narrator and a label would say the same thing, cut one** — because Mayer's redundancy principle penalises duplicate text, and Netflix removes text subtitles that repeat what is already on screen. The caption track already shows the narration, so a label that repeats it appears twice. [61, 56 · E/P] (roles: writer | checks)
- **Write lines that also work when read: no meaning carried only by tone (sarcasm, irony), no puns that depend on sound, no "listen to this"** — because the muted viewer reads the narration rather than hears it. NowThis designed its text to read the same with sound on or off. [62 · I] (roles: writer | checks)

**Anti-patterns (don't)**

- **Don't plan around "80–85% watch on mute" as a current fact for Reels, TikTok or Shorts** — because those figures come from 2016 Facebook-feed self-reports and vendor blogs, while Meta now reports most Reels plays are sound-on. Design for both audiences instead. [46, 49, 50 · E/F] (roles: writer | director)
- **Don't run captions that paraphrase the voice** — because a mismatch between voice and caption reads as an error to anyone hearing both. [55 · I] (roles: checks)
- **Don't flash single words in rapid succession or over-animate the caption** — because nothing peer-reviewed supports it, and breaking phrases apart raises cognitive load. [59 · E/I] (roles: director | checks)
- **Don't put a full transcript card on screen while the narrator reads it and captions run** — because that stacks the same words two or three times and competes with the picture. [61 · E] (roles: writer | director)
- **Don't let the payoff or joke ride only on sound** — because muted viewers miss it. [I] (roles: writer | director)
