# 01 — Documentary directing, journalism, and premium-doc craft (the director's brain)

Desk research for the AI Mini Docs format (60-second vertical mini-documentaries built from real photos and
documents). Compiled 2026-10-28. It is written to be distilled into the WRITER and DIRECTOR craft files.

**How to read this file**
- Each line is one principle: the move, why it works, the source number (see the list at the end), and the
  roles it applies to.
- Roles: `director` (story and visual intent), `writer` (script and voiceover), `editor` (cut, pacing, motion on
  stills), `sound` (score, foley, mix), `scout` (finding and vetting photos, documents and posts), `checks`
  (verification, fairness and integrity review).
- Every principle is paraphrased in my own words; no source is quoted verbatim. Where a primary page could not be
  opened and the point comes from a secondary reproduction or search excerpt, the source entry says so.
  "(synthesis)" marks a principle I derived from several sources for the 60-second format rather than one any
  source states outright.
- Named examples appear only where the cited source itself uses them, and are marked "Example (source)".

## 1. The story engine: character, want, obstacle, stakes

- **Find the person inside the event, then name what they want and what blocks them** — because audiences connect through one relatable figure's motives and struggles, and story is conflict that rises toward some resolution. [7, 2] (roles: writer | director)
- **Put a human cost on the stakes** — because one person's fate hits harder than an institution's fall. Example (source): Gibney opened his film about a corporate collapse on one executive's death, not on the company. [15] (roles: writer | director)
- **Frame one dramatic question that the piece answers, or honestly leaves open** — because a simple throughline is what keeps dense factual material coherent without forcing a formula on it. [1, 2] (roles: writer)
- **Draft a working hypothesis before scripting: what you believe, what will show it, and through whom** — because Rabiger's hypothesis prompts force a point of view and a cast before material is chosen. [5] (roles: writer | director)
- **Hold that hypothesis loosely and let the evidence overturn it** — because the direct-cinema tradition casts the filmmaker as someone who discovers rather than controls, and Kopple embedded without knowing how her stories would end. [16, 17] (roles: writer | checks)
- **Find the theme that is bigger than the incident** — because Op-Docs looks for a universal idea beneath the single story, and Bernard says a story gains reach when it rises above its specifics. [8, 2] (roles: writer)
- **Reach the heart first and the head second** — because, per course notes on Bernard, films work on emotion before intellect, so feeling is what carries the facts in. [3] (roles: writer | editor)

## 2. Structure, acts and turning points in real events

- **Give the material a concrete frame with a built-in end point** — because the Tiger King editors said the murder-for-hire plot is what let them contain a sprawling world in a finite shape. [20] (roles: writer | editor)
- **Choose the inciting incident from the real chronology on purpose** — because the classic arc (setup, inciting incident, rising action, climax, resolution) is laid over real events by selection; it is not handed to you. [7] (roles: writer)
- **Treat the point of attack, where the film enters the story, as a major decision** — because Bernard sees it as the doorway into the film's world, themes and characters, and it is among the hardest calls to make. [4] (roles: writer | director)
- **Let real outcomes rebuild the structure** — because when a verdict went against expectations, the editors of Sean Combs: The Reckoning re-centred the whole series on it. [22] (roles: editor | writer)
- **Plan a turn that deepens understanding, not just a shock** — because MediaStorm values a surprise that deepens the viewer's grasp of the character, and a change or realization as the emotional peak. [7] (roles: writer)
- **Keep the forward drive and let exposition ride on it** — because films move through time, and Bernard argues the telling should move forward too, with exposition arriving only when the story needs it. [3] (roles: writer | editor)
- **Cut favorite material that does not serve the arc, and make every shot support the argument** — because the Tiger King editors found dropping beloved scenes the hardest part, and compared the edit to a term paper where every shot serves the thesis. [20] (roles: editor | writer | director)

## 3. Controlling information: hooks, cold opens, reveals

- **Treat the hook as the film's promise about what it is, not its loudest fact** — because Bernard notes the hook often settles last, once theme and character are clear. [1] (roles: writer | director)
- **Reveal the outcome early when the real question is how or why** — because suspense can move from what happened to how it happened. Example (source): Bernard notes that Jonestown announces the deaths at the start, yet its hook is an insider's view of joining. [1] (roles: writer)
- **Use the cold open to start something moving at the height of the stakes, and to teach the film's visual language** — because The Perfect Neighbor's editor chose its open for stakes and to get viewers used to bodycam footage, and the Greatest Night in Pop editor says an open must state themes more plainly than later scenes. [23, 24] (roles: director | editor)
- **Build a cold open whose meaning flips by the end** — because the Sean Combs: The Reckoning editors reprised their opening phone call late in the series, where the same moment reads as its opposite. [22] (roles: editor | writer)
- **Prefer suspense to surprise: let viewers know something is coming, but not when** — because Hitchcock contrasted a brief jolt with sustained suspense when the audience is informed, and that is how tension built across The Perfect Neighbor's calls. [43, 23] (roles: writer | editor | sound)
- **Delay gratification: show first, explain after** — because Bricca's fifth principle uses visual reveals to provoke curiosity before satisfying it. [6] (roles: editor | writer)
- **Plant a mystery so the viewer starts investigating** — because Gibney says a sense of mystery turns viewers into investigators, and that engagement carries the denser material. [15] (roles: writer | director)
- **Make every beat add new information** — because The Perfect Neighbor's editor cut repetitive calls so each one moved the story on. [23] (roles: editor)
- **Let the evidence itself deliver the reveal** — because Bricca's "favor firsthand experience" principle aims to put the audience as near to direct experience as the material allows. [6] (roles: editor | scout)

## 4. Endings and "what happened next"

- **End on a scene or an artifact, not a summary** — because The Perfect Neighbor's editor built its ending as a scene with credits over it and explicitly not as a montage. [23] (roles: editor | writer)
- **Make the last beat a springboard to the next real question** — because premium docuseries editors design episode endings to pull viewers forward, and Chimp Crazy's supervising editor counts its cliffhangers among the best of his career. [22, 21] (roles: writer | editor)
- **Make the cliffhanger true: end on a documented, genuinely open next step** — because an ending that implies more than the record supports gets called misleading. Example (source): Bad Vegan's subject publicly disputed its closing phone call as misleading, saying it dated from earlier. [26] (roles: writer | checks)

## 5. Perspective, competing voices and unreliable narrators

- **Decide whose point of view the piece takes, and hold it** — because defining point of view is one of Bricca's ten editing principles. [6] (roles: director | writer)
- **When the record is contested, let each side speak in its own words and let viewers weigh it** — because the Wild Wild Country team chose early not to take sides, showing events as each group lived them and leaving the verdict to the audience. [18, 19] (roles: writer | editor)
- **Expose an unreliable narrator with contradicting evidence, not with insinuation** — because Bad Vegan's late turn against its own narrator drew a public charge that it misled. [26] (roles: writer | editor | checks)
- **Re-live events rather than look back on them** — because Wild Wild Country's editor wanted viewers to feel present inside the history, not reviewing it from today. [19] (roles: director | editor)
- **Let the record narrate: texts, posts, recordings, documents** — because American Murder was built from social posts, police recordings, texts and home video, with no talking heads. [29] (roles: director | scout)
- **For confrontation or confession beats, prefer images where the subject meets the lens** — because Morris built the Interrotron so subjects address the viewer directly, which he says creates a true first person. [13] (roles: scout | director)
- **Center the people harmed, not the perpetrator's self-image** — because The Tinder Swindler team decided the film was not about the con man and refused to give his story a platform. [32] (roles: writer | director)

## 6. Premium-doc grammar and tone

- **Layer the tone: gripping, funny and poignant can coexist** — because the Chimp Crazy editors aimed for all three, knowing the series only has impact if viewers stay. [21] (roles: director | editor)
- **Make difficult figures watchable without endorsing them** — because Chimp Crazy's editors tried to make troubling people relatable or at least compelling, while balancing that against the harm they caused. [21] (roles: writer | director)
- **Entertain hard without punishing the audience** — because the same editors argue that a message does not need cruelty to land; accuracy plus entertainment does the work. [21] (roles: director | editor)
- **Match the visual style to the subject** — because Gibney argues style should reflect content. Example (source): Enron's reflective surfaces and mirrored interviewees echo a fraud's illusion. [15] (roles: director)
- **Give each recurring source type one consistent visual treatment** — because Gibney lit and framed one group of interviewees identically so viewers would instantly place them. [15] (roles: director | editor)
- **Escalate: each beat should raise the stakes or tighten the question** — because rising tension toward a resolution is what turns a sequence of facts into story. [2] (roles: writer | editor)
- **Keep on-screen graphics restrained so type never upstages the event** — because designers on a recent true-crime series chose restrained graphics out of respect for the material. [37] (roles: director | editor)

## 7. Score and sound as a tension engine

- **Score what is happening inside the people, not the action on screen** — because O.J.: Made in America's composer scored the subjects' inner lives and left guilt or innocence in the subtext. [38] (roles: sound)
- **Practice restraint, and hold the line between neutral and tense** — because documentary composers name restraint as a core hurdle, and a Frontline composer warns against melodrama that gives the emotion away. [39, 40] (roles: sound)
- **Don't let the score take sides in a contested story** — because Wild Wild Country's score reflects each interviewee's outlook instead of judging them. [42] (roles: sound | director)
- **Use thriller pulse for momentum, then switch to a mourning register for real loss** — because true-crime composers told Variety they use thriller technique freely but write elegy for grief, mindful that victims' families will watch. [41] (roles: sound)
- **Build tension from sounds native to the story** — because Nainita Desai turned the sound of fingers tapping phone screens into percussion in American Murder. [31] (roles: sound)
- **Let the world's own sound carry scenes before music** — because The Perfect Neighbor kept score out until the night of the event, so the footage stayed raw. [23] (roles: sound | editor)
- **Give stills a full sound world: ambience, foley, period detail** — because Burns describes layering a feature-grade sound-effects track under his still photographs (wording unverified). [10] (roles: sound)
- **Never add or alter sound so it implies an event the record does not show** — because the NPPA code forbids audio changes that could mislead viewers or misrepresent subjects. [45] (roles: sound | checks)

## 8. Visual grammar for stills and archival material

- **Shoot each photo like a scene: wide, medium, close, reveal** — because Burns treats a still the way a feature director treats coverage. [9] (roles: director | editor)
- **Move only when the move means something** — because Burns rejects restless motion for its own sake and keeps only moves that carry meaning. [9] (roles: editor | director)
- **Let key images hold** — because Burns argues meaning builds over time spent with an image, not in the first glance. [9] (roles: editor)
- **Treat the first and last frame of every move as an editorial claim** — because the photo editors Poynter interviewed note a move's starting point chooses the detail for the viewer and can shift what the photo means. [9] (roles: editor | checks)
- **Time a tilt or pan so the detail appears when the line needs it** — because a slow reveal steers the eye. Example (source): Burns describes a tilt that uncovers a gun tucked into a waistband. [9] (roles: editor | director)
- **Use 2.5D parallax sparingly, on clean separations, and never to invent content** — because the layered technique popularized by The Kid Stays in the Picture requires filling the gaps behind cut-out layers with pixels nobody photographed. [35] (synthesis with 46) (roles: editor | checks)
- **Bridge pieces of evidence with quiet establishing images** — because The Perfect Neighbor's editor used near-invisible bridge shots so recorded audio could carry the story over imagery. [23] (roles: editor | scout)
- **Run original audio over archive instead of cutting to present-day talking heads** — because Senna kept every interview off camera, playing the voices over period footage. [34] (roles: director | editor)
- **Let messages and posts drive the narrative, and keep secondary detail secondary** — because American Murder's designers tested how texts could drive the story and kept surrounding social-post detail subordinate to the key moment. [30] (roles: director | editor)
- **Show change over time with matched before-and-after frames** — because rephotography registers old and new images from the same vantage so the change reads at once. [36] (roles: scout | editor)
- **Choose the primary artifact over an illustration of it** — because the Archival Producers Alliance builds its guidance around protecting primary sources, and Bricca ranks footage by its closeness to direct experience. [52, 6] (roles: scout | director)

## 9. Sourcing, verification and attribution

- **Verify every visual for provenance, source, date, location and motivation, provenance first; check how it was made, not only what it claims** — because First Draft ranks provenance as the most important check, and SPJ asks journalists to verify the methods and technology behind material. [53, 44] (roles: scout | checks)
- **Confirm each factual claim against two credible sources** — because course notes on Bernard report this as her research standard. [3] (roles: checks | writer)
- **Name the source on screen whenever it affects belief** — because SPJ asks journalists to identify clearly the sources that shape a story. [44] (roles: writer | editor | checks)
- **Keep a source log for every image, quote and document** — because the APA recommends a detailed cue sheet for synthetic elements, and Op-Docs fact-checks every film to Times standards. [52, 8] (roles: scout | checks)
- **Document the fair-use reasoning for every third-party image** — because CMSI's statement grounds documentary fair use in four situations (critique, illustrating a point, incidental capture, historical sequence), each with limits. [51] (roles: scout | checks)
- **Disclose conflicts of interest and paid access** — because rival coverage exposed both. Example (source): Netflix's Fyre was co-produced by the festival's own marketing agency, while Hulu's rival team paid the organizer for an interview. [33] (roles: checks | director)
- **Hold short pieces to the full standard** — because SPJ says no speed, technology or format excuses inaccuracy or unfairness. [44] (roles: checks)
- **Fix errors quickly and visibly** — because SPJ asks journalists to own mistakes and correct them promptly and prominently. [44] (roles: checks)

## 10. Fairness, right of reply, minimizing harm

- **Give anyone facing allegations a fair chance to respond, and reflect the reply** — because SPJ treats this as an obligation without exceptions, and BBC guideline 6.2.3 normally requires a right of reply. [44, 48] (roles: writer | checks)
- **Keep fact, allegation and inference distinct in the words themselves** — because SPJ asks that commentary be labeled, and an allegation is not a finding. [44] (roles: writer | checks)
- **Show compassion and refuse lurid detail, even if competitors use it** — because SPJ warns against feeding lurid curiosity and asks for compassion toward those affected. [44] (roles: writer | director)
- **Don't leave vulnerable subjects worse off than you found them** — because filmmakers in CMSI's Honest Truths study named protecting vulnerable subjects as a shared obligation. [50] (roles: director | checks)
- **Remember that a public post is not a license** — because SPJ separates legal access to information from the ethical case for publishing it. [44] (roles: scout | checks)

## 11. Image ethics: what may be done to a real photo

- **Limit photo edits to technical ones that keep content intact: crop, tone, color correction, dust and scratch removal** — because AP permits only these, and only within limits that preserve the photo's authentic nature. [46] (roles: editor | checks)
- **Never add, remove, move or clone anything inside a real photo** — because Reuters bars staging and moving objects, and called deliberate manipulation its gravest photo breach after its 2006 Lebanon scandal. [47] (roles: editor | checks)
- **Crop and frame without changing what the picture says** — because Morris shows a frame can exclude what would change a photo's meaning, and NPPA asks that editing keep content and context intact. [11, 45] (roles: editor | scout)
- **Treat the caption as the claim, and get date, place and event exactly right** — because Morris argues photographs are neither true nor false; only statements about them are. [11, 12] (roles: writer | checks)
- **Make cutouts, markups, maps and code-built elements read unmistakably as graphics** — because BBC guidance says unlabelled reconstructions must look visibly different, Op-Docs requires recreations to be obvious, and SPJ says to label illustrations. [48, 8, 44] (roles: director | editor | checks)
- **Assume any photo may be staged, and label handouts and posed images as such** — because Morris holds that every photograph involves some staging and that famous ones attract claims of fakery. [11] (synthesis) (roles: scout | checks)
- **Pull an image whose integrity is in doubt** — because agencies withdrew a royal family photo in 2024 for failing their standards, while stating it was not fake. Example (source): the Princess of Wales family photo. [46] (roles: checks)

## 12. Accuracy when compressing

- **Never join separate moments of speech into one continuous statement** — because BBC rules bar edits that leave a materially misleading impression. Example (source): a 2025 Panorama splice of a political speech forced a review and drew a lawsuit. [49] (roles: editor | checks)
- **Keep quoted lines in their true order, or mark the jump** — because reordering manufactures meaning. Example (source): The Jinx's closing lines aired out of order, placed for dramatic effect, as the NYT later reported. [25] (roles: editor | checks)
- **Don't let juxtaposition assert what the evidence does not** — because BBC rules prohibit intercutting whose combination distorts meaning. [49] (roles: editor | checks)
- **Treat every omission as an editorial claim** — because omissions become the story once contested. Example (source): Making a Murderer's producers called omitted evidence insignificant; the prosecutor disagreed. [27] (roles: writer | checks)

## 13. Compressing a documentary into 60 seconds

- **Tell one story through one main figure, not a survey of experts** — because Op-Docs favors a focused story over films built on many experts or argument. [8] (roles: writer)
- **Make every beat need a picture** — because Op-Docs requires that the reason a piece is a video be obvious. [8] (roles: director | writer)
- **Win the opening seconds** — because Op-Docs wants a strong start, since online viewers leave easily. [8] (roles: writer | director | editor)
- **Simplify ruthlessly, but never past the truth** — because Bricca's last principle is to simplify, while SPJ requires accuracy in context. [6, 44] (roles: writer | editor | checks)
- **Keep only the backstory that the turn requires** — because forward drive and the discipline of cutting favorites both push context down to the one fact that makes the twist land. (synthesis of 3, 20) (roles: writer | editor)

## Anti-patterns (don't)

- **Don't move a still just to keep the frame busy** — because Burns calls restless, meaningless motion a disease of imagery. [9] (roles: editor | director)
- **Don't stitch separate statements together, or reorder them, for a better line** — because both the Panorama splice and The Jinx reordering became scandals about the edit itself. [49, 25] (roles: editor | checks)
- **Don't cut to an unrelated image that implies guilt or causation** — because BBC rules treat distorting juxtaposition as a breach even when every clip is real. [49] (roles: editor | checks)
- **Don't add a sound that implies an event the record doesn't show** — because NPPA treats misleading audio like a manipulated photo. [45] (roles: sound | checks)
- **Don't retouch content, or blur or burn down a real photo's background to steer the eye** — because AP forbids background blurring and toning that alters the scene. [46] (roles: editor | checks)
- **Don't caption a photo with a time, place or event it doesn't show** — because the caption is where photographic deception mostly happens. [11, 12] (roles: writer | checks)
- **Don't pass off a reconstruction, cutout or code-built graphic as found evidence** — because every standard reviewed requires reconstructions to be labeled or visibly distinct. [48, 8, 44] (roles: director | editor | checks)
- **Don't end on an implication the record doesn't support** — because a misleading final beat invites public dispute from the subject. [26] (roles: writer | checks)
- **Don't hand the perpetrator the microphone** — because centering the con artist turns the film into his platform. [32] (roles: writer | director)
- **Don't let the score deliver the verdict** — because melodrama gives the emotion away and takes a side the evidence may not support. [40, 42] (roles: sound)
- **Don't stack expert talking points in place of a story** — because short-doc editors reject films built on argument instead of a character. [8] (roles: writer)
- **Don't use a famous or viral image without tracing its origin** — because the most famous photos attract the most staging claims, and provenance is the first check. [11, 53] (roles: scout | checks)
- **Don't hide paid access or a producer's stake in the story** — because undisclosed conflicts become the story. [33] (roles: checks | director)

## Where the field disagrees

- **Reenactment and stylization.** Morris argues that no style guarantees truth and defended reenactment against critics of The Thin Blue Line; the BBC, SPJ and Op-Docs require reconstructions to be labeled or visibly different, and Reuters bars staging outright. The working consensus for this format: drawn or code-built elements are fine, provided no one could mistake them for evidence. [14, 48, 44, 8, 47]
- **Neutral versus argued.** Wild Wild Country declined to pick sides and kept rival groups in separate stretches rather than intercutting them as if they were debating; Gibney treats documentary as authored nonfiction with a shaped aesthetic; composer Serj Tankian told Variety he would likely fail a director who wanted neutral music. [18, 15, 41]
- **Should a still move?** Burns animates photos when the move carries meaning; a photo editor in the same Poynter piece would leave a powerful image untouched; another says motion is not inherently unethical but can change how reality is perceived. [9]
- **Archive-only versus talking heads.** Senna's producers had planned on interviews, but the first editor pushed for archive only, and the film plays its interviews as audio alone. Critics are split on whether that is a new form or the same form with the faces removed. [34]
- **Selection and omission.** Making a Murderer's directors argue a documentary cannot contain a whole trial; the prosecutor argues the omissions decided the viewer's verdict. [27]
- **Implicating the audience.** Don't F**k with Cats closes by turning the question of complicity on viewers; some critics call it brilliant, others call it a cheap rebuke. [28]
- **Spoil or withhold?** Bernard's Jonestown example gives away the ending and still holds; Hitchcock's rule to inform the audience allows an exception when a twist ending is the point. [1, 43]
- **Dramatic placement versus literal order.** The Jinx's editor said moving a line to the finale was for drama and not meant to link it to another remark; critics argue that reassembling the words produced the meaning. [25]
