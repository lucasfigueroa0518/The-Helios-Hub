# 05 — Story development: finding, vetting and choosing stories at scale

Track: how documentary producers, narrative journalists and business-story creators find, vet and choose stories, and how an LLM "development producer" can do the same at scale without repeating itself.

Researched 2026-10-28 (web search plus page fetches). Format of each principle:
`- **Do X** — because Y. [S#] [evidence | practice | lore] (roles: …)`

- **evidence**: a study with data, or a published standard.
- **practice**: stated working practice of named professionals or institutions.
- **lore**: creator self-description, secondary summaries or folk wisdom. Treat it as a hint, not a rule.

Roles: **producer** (generates pitches), **drama-judge** (scores dramatic potential), **material-judge** (scores whether real visual material exists), **ledger** (records what has been explored and pitched).

There are no AI-industry story examples in this file. The few illustrative examples come from the sources and are marked *(example)*.

---

## 1. Is there a story here? (story, not topic)

- **Do require a protagonist, a complication and a resolution before calling anything a story** — because narrative editors treat "protagonist-complication-resolution" as the minimum unit of story. A subject with no arc is a "flat" A-to-Z explainer. [S18][S19] [practice] (roles: producer, drama-judge)
- **Do reject pitches that are news or topics rather than stories** — because Jack Hart says writers miss narrative because "we mistake news for story". A significant event is not yet a scene. [S18] [practice] (roles: producer, drama-judge)
- **Do look for people solving a problem, not for important subjects** — because Hart's practical lens is to find "folks who solve problems": a challenge, a change in thinking, and an outcome. *(example: Tom Hallman Jr.'s piece on a woman training with a seeing-eye dog)* [S18] [practice] (roles: producer)
- **Do demand three things in every pitch: a person to relate to, a surprising turn, and an insight that follows from it** — because this is Ira Glass's repeated test for This American Life stories, and surprise without insight fails it. [S20] [practice] (roles: producer, drama-judge)
- **Do test that the surprise is real, not big** — because Glass says the subject need not be a big thing, only a surprising one: a moment someone would excitedly retell. [S20] [practice] (roles: drama-judge)
- **Do pair each anecdote (sequence of actions) with a moment of reflection (what it means)** — because Glass describes these as the two building blocks of radio story. For Reel AI Drama, they map to the "scene" and to the "implication for the AI world". [S20] [practice] (roles: producer)
- **Do write the expected shape as "compelling beginning, unexpected middle, satisfying end"** — because Bernard's test of a documentary story is structural, and the unexpected middle is where pitches usually go soft. [S1][S2] [practice] (roles: producer, drama-judge)
- **Do look for two stories unfolding at once, involving people in conflict** — because Jon Else (quoted via Bernard) uses this as his test of a film-worthy narrative. Two lines create cross-cutting and tension. [S2] [practice] (roles: producer, drama-judge)
- **Do choose the "point of attack" (where the story enters) during development, not later** — because Bernard treats the entry point as one of the hardest and most consequential decisions. In 60 seconds, it is the whole hook. [S2] [practice] (roles: producer)
- **Do frame each pitch as a hypothesis to be verified, not a fact** — because Story-Based Inquiry defines the story as a provisional hypothesis based on available evidence. It stays a hypothesis until confirmed. [S21] [practice] (roles: producer, material-judge)

## 2. Character tests

- **Do name a specific protagonist with an active want** — because Hart's protagonist actively works on the central problem. Commissioners repeatedly favour "character-driven" work. [S19][S8][S7] [practice] (roles: producer, drama-judge)
- **Do state the stakes and the conflict explicitly** — because pitch-forum reports say a documentary needs conflict and stakes to hold a commissioner. [S7] [practice] (roles: producer, drama-judge)
- **Do score whether the audience can understand or sympathise with the protagonist, not just admire them** — because reports from the IDFA forum note that likability (or at least relatability) of the main character mattered consistently. Glass's first requirement is someone to relate to. [S7][S20] [practice] (roles: drama-judge)
- **Do prefer an obscure but documented person at a decisive moment over a famous person at a routine one** — because Hart argues a story can involve someone of little obvious consequence. The drama is in the arc, not the fame. [S18] [practice] (roles: producer, drama-judge)

## 3. Why now and why care

- **Do answer "why would anyone want to know?" and "why now?" in one line each** — because both appear in commissioning guidance for short docs. For an evergreen subject, editors want the timely peg made explicit. [S6][S22] [practice] (roles: producer, drama-judge)
- **Do give a wider reason to care beyond the subject itself** — because Shelley Jones (Nowness) says to "find a wider reason for why people should care". In Lucas's terms, this is the implication for the AI world. [S5] [practice] (roles: producer, drama-judge)
- **Do find a theme that applies to the viewer's own life** — because Hart says the theme should matter to readers, not only to the protagonist. [S18] [practice] (roles: producer)
- **Do favour evergreen angles over breaking news** — because Acquired deliberately avoids breaking news to keep episodes "evergreen", which suits a back-catalogue format. [S26] [lore] (roles: producer)

## 4. Access, material and feasibility tests (before any resources are spent)

- **Do treat access as part of the idea, not a later production step** — because commissioners at IDFA singled out unique, intimate access. Nowness's commissioner lists existing access as a top criterion. For an archive-led format, "access" means the record exists. [S7][S5] [practice] (roles: material-judge)
- **Do ask "can this actually be pulled off?" before scoring drama** — because feasibility is a separate commissioning test ("make sure this is something you can actually pull off"). A great story with no material is a dead pitch. [S5] [practice] (roles: material-judge)
- **Do scope the specific visual material needed at the pitch stage** — because archive guidance says to assess precise archive requirements as early as possible, and there is little room for wasted research once production starts. [S12] [practice] (roles: material-judge)
- **Do follow the standard archive sequence: need → identify candidate material → contact or check likely holders to confirm it exists → let editorial evaluate** — because this is the UK National Occupational Standard for obtaining archive material. [S14] [evidence: published standard] (roles: material-judge)
- **Do check licensability and cost, not just existence** — because the archive producer must confirm material is licensable for the project's terms and within budget. Existing but unusable material does not count. [S13] [practice] (roles: material-judge)
- **Do record a fallback when the key image doesn't exist** — because archive producers are expected to propose workable alternatives. Bernard notes animation or reconstruction as options, provided the audience knows. [S13][S3] [practice] (roles: material-judge)
- **Do score material on provenance: who made it, why, for whom, and does it match other authoritative research** — because Bernard warns that archival material is not always what happened. Blackside used archive as evidence, subject to fact-checking. [S3][S4] [practice] (roles: material-judge)
- **Do distinguish archive-as-evidence from archive-as-illustration** — because using a generic or mismatched image as if it showed the event misleads, and Bernard says fabricated material must not be passed off as authentic. [S3] [practice] (roles: material-judge)
- **Do not lock in paid or high-res assets before the story survives editing** — because archive practitioners warn that clips you love often get cut. Verify existence early and license late. [S15] [practice] (roles: material-judge)
- **Do expect each outlet to fact-check and standards-review every film** — because Op-Docs states every film is fact-checked and reviewed for standards before publication. A pitch should carry its sources from the start. [S9] [practice] (roles: producer, material-judge)

## 5. Pitch and logline format

- **Do state the idea in one or two sentences** — because commissioner panels' first tip is a 1–2 sentence description. Screen Australia notes that preparing the pitch clarifies story, character and theme. [S16][S6] [practice] (roles: producer)
- **Do build the logline around irony (a built-in contradiction) and a compelling mental picture** — because Snyder names these as the first two logline elements. The irony raises "how is that going to work?", and the picture implies the whole film. [S17] [practice] (roles: producer, drama-judge)
- **Do include the concrete scene image in the pitch itself** — because a logline must "bloom in your mind". Lucas's criterion 2 (a vivid moment that makes it a scene) is the documentary version of the mental-picture test. [S17] [practice] (roles: producer, material-judge)
- **Do pitch only what fits the outlet's priorities and style** — because commissioners say each outlet has its own priorities, and misfit pitches are the most common waste. For an LLM producer, the "outlet" is the series format spec. [S5][S10] [practice] (roles: producer)
- **Do keep pitch quality and film quality as separate judgments** — because a commissioner warned that a great pitch is not necessarily a great documentary. The drama judge should score the underlying event, not the prose of the pitch. (unverified; commissioner remark seen in search results, original not located) [practice] (roles: drama-judge)

## 6. Sourcing ideas: beats, records, slates

- **Do mine primary records (court documents, registries, filings, lawsuits, published histories) as idea sources** — because investigative trainers say there are hundreds of stories in court and business records, and ideas often start from a curious passage in a document. [S25][S24] [practice] (roles: producer)
- **Do keep one running idea list and test each item for gravity and downside before pursuing it** — because newsroom trainers recommend a single idea log tested on how grave the matter is and what happens if it falls apart. [S24] [practice] (roles: ledger, drama-judge)
- **Do maintain a slate or backlog of candidates rather than generating on demand** — because Acquired reportedly works from a shared doc of 50+ candidate topics, many from listeners (unverified). [S27] [lore] (roles: ledger)
- **Do read everything already published before pitching** — because Acquired's hosts each read all available published material, filings and primary sources (about 100 hours per episode). IJNet tells editors to check prior clips to separate new from old. [S26][S22] [practice] (roles: producer, ledger)
- **Do build stories with timelines and stakeholder maps** — because Story-Based Inquiry uses timelines and stakeholder maps as the building blocks that turn a hypothesis into scenes. [S21] [practice] (roles: producer)
- **Do use a recurring format frame (one rivalry per season; one biography per episode) to generate a predictable idea space** — because Business Wars runs one corporate rivalry per multi-episode season *(example: Netflix vs Blockbuster)*, and Founders runs "one biography at a time". [S28][S29] [lore] (roles: producer)
- **Do use story meetings to decide by explicit news values** — because research on newsroom story meetings shows editors invoke shared news values to settle disagreements. Explicit criteria make choices auditable. [S23] [evidence] (roles: drama-judge)

## 7. Greenlight, scale down, or kill

- **Do probe a pitch with questions before approving or rejecting it** — because IJNet advises editors to ask about cost, feasibility, timeliness and the news peg instead of answering yes or no outright. [S22] [practice] (roles: drama-judge, material-judge)
- **Do consider a smaller version before killing a promising idea** — because editors are advised to run a scaled-down story now when the peg is strong. For reels, that could mean a single moment instead of a whole saga. [S22] [practice] (roles: drama-judge)
- **Do record the reason for every kill or "not now", and what would revive it** — because IJNet tells editors to say exactly why they pass and what must change first. In a ledger, that is a structured kill reason plus a revive condition. [S22] [practice] (roles: ledger)
- **Do ask "what is the downside if this falls apart?" for each pitch** — because it surfaces stories resting on a single unverified claim. [S24] [practice] (roles: drama-judge, material-judge)

## 8. LLM ideation: why it repeats itself

- **Do assume an unguided LLM will converge on the same few famous ideas** — because Si et al. found only about 200 non-duplicate ideas out of 4,000 generated seeds per topic, with new-batch novelty falling until it plateaus. [S31] [evidence] (roles: producer, ledger)
- **Do not expect more samples to buy more variety** — because the same study concludes that scaling generation mostly yields repeats. Diversity has to be engineered, not sampled into existence. [S31] [evidence] (roles: producer)
- **Do expect different models to converge too, not only one model with itself** — because "Artificial Hivemind" documents intra-model repetition and inter-model homogeneity on open-ended prompts. Switching models is not a diversity strategy. [S33] [evidence] (roles: producer)
- **Do expect bigger, newer models to be less diverse by default** — because NoveltyBench found all 20 frontier models lacked diversity, and larger models in a family were often less diverse. [S37] [evidence] (roles: producer)
- **Do treat mode collapse as a typicality bias** — because Zhang et al. trace it to preference data in which annotators favour familiar text. The model's default pitch is the most typical story, i.e. the one everyone has already told. [S32] [evidence] (roles: producer, drama-judge)
- **Do expect AI-assisted ideas to be individually better but collectively narrower** — because Doshi & Hauser's experiment found AI-sourced story ideas rated more creative while making the stories more similar to each other. [S35] [evidence] (roles: producer, ledger)

## 9. LLM ideation: methods that increase diversity

- **Do drive generation from an explicit grid (player × story type × era), one cell per call** — because Quality-Diversity methods fill an archive of defined niches and find more distinct high-quality outputs than optimising quality alone. [S38] [evidence] (roles: producer, ledger)
- **Do ask for a distribution of several candidates with estimated probabilities, then sample from the tail** — because Verbalized Sampling (a list of responses plus probabilities) recovers much of the base model's diversity. A probability threshold makes the diversity tunable. [S32] [evidence] (roles: producer)
- **Do make the model reason step by step through the cell before pitching** — because chain-of-thought produced the most diverse idea pools in Meincke et al., and Deng et al. find CoT reduces LLM fixation on early outputs. [S34][S36] [evidence] (roles: producer)
- **Do use ordinary, varied personas or vantage points (e.g. a junior engineer, a rival's lawyer, a reporter covering it), not famous-genius personas** — because Deng et al. find ordinary personas partition knowledge better than "creative entrepreneur" personas, and CoT plus personas gave the highest diversity. [S36] [evidence] (roles: producer)
- **Do expect diversity gains from any single prompt trick to fade over long runs** — because one secondary summary reports the CoT advantage disappearing after about 750–800 ideas (unverified). The ledger and the grid must carry the load over time. [S34] [lore] (roles: ledger)
- **Do not put famous worked examples in the generation prompt** — because LLMs fixate on early or shown content [S36], and typical content is preferred [S32]. Examples get copied, so describe the qualities instead. No direct study isolating few-shot pollution in ideation was found. [S36][S32] [evidence, indirect] (roles: producer)
- **Do use an LLM to judge both quality and novelty against the archive, not quality alone** — because QDAIF uses LM feedback for both quality and diversity axes, and a fitness-only baseline failed to diversify. [S38] [evidence] (roles: drama-judge, ledger)

## 10. Deduplication and the ever-growing ledger

- **Do deduplicate at the level of the real-world event, not the wording of the pitch** — because two pitches about the same event can be phrased entirely differently. Cross-document event coreference treats "same real-world event" as the unit. [S40][S41] [evidence] (roles: ledger)
- **Do store a structured event fingerprint per pitch (who, what action, when, where, the key document or moment)** — because event-coreference systems compare event arguments and context, and a fingerprint lets exact-match checks run cheaply before any model call. [S40][S41] [practice, derived] (roles: ledger)
- **Do use embedding similarity as a first-pass filter with a tuned threshold** — because Si et al. flagged duplicates at cosine above 0.8, and SemDeDup removes semantic near-duplicates by clustering and then thresholding within clusters. Thresholds are dataset-specific, so tune on your own labelled pairs. [S31][S39] [evidence] (roles: ledger)
- **Do send only borderline pairs to an LLM same-event judge, after summarising each mention** — because Min et al. improve event coreference by having an LLM summarise each mention's context before the same-event decision. Running the LLM on every pair is wasteful. [S40] [evidence] (roles: ledger)
- **Do not rely on keyword search to decide whether something was already covered** — because novelty checkers built on keyword retrieval miss relevant prior items that don't share the exact terms. [S44][S46] [evidence] (roles: ledger)
- **Do retrieve only the nearest prior pitches for the current grid cell into the prompt, never the whole ledger** — because model accuracy drops for information in the middle of long contexts and as contexts grow. A dumped ledger stops working as it grows. [S42] [evidence] (roles: ledger, producer)
- **Do mark each grid cell's state (unexplored / explored-empty / pitched / killed-with-reason / produced)** — because Quality-Diversity archives work by tracking which niches are filled, and editors need kill reasons to avoid re-pitching. [S38][S22] [practice] (roles: ledger)
- **Do log searches that found nothing, not just pitches** — because the ledger's purpose is that nothing is searched twice. An empty cell is information. Si et al.'s plateau shows that repeats are the default without it. [S31] [practice, derived] (roles: ledger)

## 11. Judging drama and material

- **Do score with an explicit rubric whose items map to named tests** — because story meetings resolve disagreement by invoking shared criteria [S23], and LLM judges are less reliable where criteria are left to preference [S33]. Suggested items, each from a source above: protagonist with a want; complication and stakes; surprise; insight or implication; scene image; why now; material exists, with provenance; feasibility. [S23][S33] [practice] (roles: drama-judge, material-judge)
- **Do keep the drama score and the material score separate** — because commissioners judge story and feasibility or access as distinct tests [S5][S7]. A blended score lets a thrilling story with no photos pass. [S5][S7] [practice] (roles: drama-judge, material-judge)
- **Do counter judge biases: swap order in pairwise comparisons, ignore length, and don't let a model grade its own pitches unchecked** — because LLM judges show position, verbosity and self-enhancement bias. [S43] [evidence] (roles: drama-judge, material-judge)
- **Do not treat an LLM judge's novelty or excitement score as ground truth** — because Si et al. found LLM evaluators unreliable as idea judges compared with expert reviewers. Novelty ratings can flip when the same idea is reworded. [S31][S44] [evidence] (roles: drama-judge)
- **Do treat "material exists" as a checkable claim with a pointer (archive, outlet, date)** — because the archive standard requires confirming existence and availability with likely holders, not assuming it. [S14] [evidence: published standard] (roles: material-judge)

## Anti-patterns (don't)

- **Don't pitch a topic or a big event with no protagonist arc.** It fails the protagonist-complication-resolution test. [S18][S19]
- **Don't pitch surprise without an insight, or insight without a scene.** Glass's test needs both. [S20]
- **Don't pitch before the material check.** Commissioners complain about projects pitched before they are ready (unverified). [S5]
- **Don't use a generic or misattributed photo as if it were evidence of the moment.** [S3]
- **Don't put famous example stories in the producer prompt.** They anchor the model and get copied. [S36][S32]
- **Don't expect sampling more, raising temperature alone, or switching models to cure repetition.** [S31][S33][S37]
- **Don't paste the whole ledger into the prompt.** Retrieve the nearest prior items for the cell. [S42]
- **Don't dedupe on keywords or wording.** Dedupe on the event. [S44][S40]
- **Don't let one LLM score its own pitches unchecked, or reward longer pitches.** [S43]
- **Don't kill silently.** Every kill needs a reason and a revive condition. [S22]
- **Don't proceed on a pitch whose whole story rests on one unverified claim.** Ask what happens if it falls apart. [S24][S21]

## A recommended development loop

1. **Pick a cell.** Choose the next unexplored or least-filled grid cell (player × story type × era) from the ledger's cell-state table. Never let the model choose freely. [S38]
2. **Retrieve the neighbourhood.** Load only the nearest prior pitches and empty searches for that cell (and adjacent cells) as a short "already covered" list of event fingerprints, not prose. [S42][S31]
3. **Research before pitching.** Search primary and published records for that cell (filings, court records, books, oral histories, contemporaneous press) and build a mini timeline and stakeholder map. Log the searches. [S21][S25][S26]
4. **Generate a distribution.** With step-by-step reasoning and two or three ordinary vantage-point personas, ask for several candidate pitches with estimated typicality, and keep the less typical ones that still pass. No example stories in the prompt. [S32][S34][S36]
5. **Write each survivor as a structured pitch.** Include protagonist and want; complication and stakes; the surprising turn; the scene image (one concrete moment); the implication; why now; an event fingerprint; and source pointers. Add a one-sentence logline with built-in irony. [S17][S18][S20]
6. **Dedupe.** Run an exact fingerprint check, then an embedding check against the ledger with a tuned threshold, then an LLM same-event judgment on borderline pairs only. [S39][S40][S31]
7. **Judge drama and material separately.** Score against a fixed rubric, with order-swapped pairwise comparisons where ranking. The material judge must cite where each key image is believed to exist and its provenance. [S43][S14][S3]
8. **Decide.** Greenlight, scale down (one moment instead of a saga), park as "not now" with a revive condition, or kill with a reason. [S22]
9. **Write everything back.** Record the cell state, searches run (including empty ones), fingerprints, scores, decisions and reasons. [S22][S38]
10. **Recalibrate.** Periodically check judge scores against Lucas's own picks on a small sample, and adjust rubric wording or thresholds. Measure ledger-wide diversity, since per-batch quality hides collective narrowing. [S31][S35]

## Where the field disagrees

- **Story first or material first?** Bernard warns against shooting first and finding the story later [S2]. Archive practice says to scope material at the very start [S12], and Johnny Harris reportedly mixes planned structure with discoveries made while reporting (lore) [S30]. For a photo-driven format, the two checks probably need to run in parallel.
- **Are stories everywhere, or rare?** Adrian Nicole LeBlanc argues almost any idea could become a story with enough time [S47]. Commissioners say strong ideas are scarce and many pitches arrive before they are ready (unverified). For an LLM producer, this becomes a question of how hard to filter versus how much to develop.
- **Personas: help or hurt?** Meincke et al. report persona modifiers help somewhat, with CoT best [S34]. Deng et al. say famous-genius personas are worse than ordinary ones [S36]. The Spark Effect practitioner report claims large gains from persona agents, but it is LLM-judged and not peer reviewed.
- **Why do models collapse?** Zhang et al. blame typicality bias in human preference data, which prompting can partly undo [S32]. "Artificial Hivemind" frames it as a cross-model ecosystem effect [S33]. NoveltyBench finds it worsening with scale [S37].
- **Can LLMs judge ideas?** Zheng et al. report strong judges reaching human-level agreement on chat quality [S43]. Si et al. and the novelty-assessment literature find them unreliable on novelty [S31][S44].
- **Is there a standard rubric for drama or archival viability?** No published, validated rubric was found. Commissioners use overlapping but informal tests (character, access, stakes, why now, feasibility, voice) [S5][S6][S7]. Archive standards cover the process, not a score [S14].
- **Breaking news or evergreen?** Newsrooms want a timely peg [S22]. Acquired deliberately avoids news to stay evergreen [S26].

## Gaps and caveats

- **Commissioning guidance.** No public, detailed "what we look for" guidance was found for Netflix or Field of Vision. Storyville's public calls are thin [S11]. POV mostly takes completed films, not pitches [S8].
- **YouTube channels.** No verifiable statements were found on how MagnatesMedia, ColdFusion, Modern MBA or Company Man choose subjects. Johnny Harris material comes from podcast-catalog summaries (lore) [S30].
- **Few-shot pollution.** No study directly isolating few-shot example pollution in LLM ideation was found. The principle rests on fixation and typicality evidence [S32][S36].
- **Pages not fully read.** Bernard's "Finding the Story" chapter, the Story-Based Inquiry manual (TCIJ page returned 403) and Hart's book were known only through reviews, interviews and summaries, not their full text.
