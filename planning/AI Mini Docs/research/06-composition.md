# 06 — Composition: building scenes from limited stills (the director's and frame worker's eye)

Desk research, 2026-10-29. Scope: how documentary editors, collage/cutout animators and motion-graphics
explainer studios compose frames when all they have is a few photos, cutouts and code-built props.
Written in answer to the owner's critique: "photo + Ken Burns + label, then the next photo + label";
the same photo on black again and again; never a composed scene (people at a table, a whiteboard
drawing of them arguing).

Complements, and does not repeat: 01 §8 (visual grammar for stills, image ethics §11) and 03 §6–9
(edit rhythm, stills/maps, captions, safe zones). Read those for move direction, markups, caption
rules and cut timing.

**How to read a line:** `Do X — because Y. [S#] (evidence | lore | proposal)`
- **evidence** = study, standard or primary documentation. **lore** = practitioner craft, unmeasured.
- **proposal** = a rule derived here from the cited craft plus the owner's critique; a starting
  default to tune, not a finding.

## 1. Build scenes, not slides

- **Stage cutouts inside a built setting (table, room, stage, street, whiteboard) whenever the beat is about people interacting** — because a relationship can only be shown by two figures sharing a space; cutout filmmakers build the whole environment (walls, furniture, props) around flat figures rather than float them. [S1][S2] (lore)
- **Ground every cutout: contact shadow, floor or table edge, overlap with a prop** — because figure-ground reads as a place only when the figure touches something; an ungrounded cutout reads as a sticker. [S3] (lore)
- **Match light, grain and colour treatment across all layers of one composite** — because mismatched edges, sharpness or tint break the illusion that the elements share one world. [S4] (lore)
- **Keep screen direction and eyelines consistent: rivals face each other across the frame and keep their sides from one frame to the next** — because the 180° convention lets viewers track who is opposing whom without labels. [S5] (lore)
- **Size each element by its importance to the story at that moment** — because Hitchcock's rule ("the size of an object in the frame should equal its importance") makes the frame argue the point before narration does. [S6] (lore)
- **Use scale contrast to show power or stakes: a small figure against a huge object, building, number or crowd** — because Block shows visual intensity rises with contrast and falls with affinity (sameness). [S7] (lore)
- **Show inner states with a drawn layer over the photo layer: scribbles, worry lines, thought balloons, arrows, a whiteboard sketch of the plan** — because simplified drawing amplifies emotion and invites identification faster than a neutral press photo ("amplification through simplification"). [S8] (lore)
- **Draw expression and caricature sketches from the anchor portrait's real features, and style them visibly as illustration** — because likeness keeps the person recognisable while the drawn style tells the viewer this is interpretation, not record (see 01 §11). [S8][S9] (lore)
- **Juxtapose only what the record supports** — because photomontage and the Kuleshov effect show that placing two images together implies a relation the viewer will believe. [S10][S11] (evidence for the effect; lore for the rule)
- **Reconstruct a reported moment (a meeting, a dinner, an argument) as a stylised tableau when no photo exists** — because animated reenactment from records is an established documentary method when the camera wasn't there (e.g. trial or eyewitness reconstructions). [S12][S13] (lore)

## 2. Depth and layering

- **Build every frame in at least three planes: setting/texture, subject, foreground element or text plate** — because separate planes give the eye depth and let the camera move with parallax instead of zooming a flat picture. [S14][S15] (lore)
- **Move planes at different rates, nearest fastest** — because that differential motion is what reads as depth. [S15] (lore)
- **Fill the background where a subject was cut out, or use a separate background plate** — because the hole behind a lifted figure shows the moment the camera moves. [S15][S16] (lore)
- **Reserve parallax for key images with clean separations; most frames should get depth from staging and layering instead** — because practitioners call blanket 2.5D a documentary cliché (see 01 §8). [S17] (lore)
- **Separate the subject from busy backgrounds with blur, darkening or desaturation of the back plane** — because one sharp, bright plane tells the eye where to land. [S7] (lore)

## 3. One anchor portrait, many treatments

- **Never show the same photo in the same treatment twice** — because repetition without change is affinity, the lowest-intensity state, and is exactly what reads as a slideshow. [S7] (lore)
- **Vary how a person appears: scale (eyes-only extreme close-up, head, full-body cutout, tiny figure in a wide scene), medium (photo, newsprint halftone, pencil trace, silhouette, on a screen, framed on a wall), and context (alone, in a group, against the opponent)** — because each treatment is a new shot of the same character, the way a camera would cover a live actor. [S8][S18] (proposal)
- **Cap one anchor portrait at about three appearances per reel, never adjacent, each in a different treatment** — because three changes give continuity of character without monotony. [owner critique][S7] (proposal)
- **Match the crop to the story moment: tight on the eyes for a decision or fear, wide for isolation or context** — because shot size sets emotional distance. [S5] (lore)

## 4. Framing the subject

- **Put the eyes on or near the upper third line and keep headroom tight** — because too much headroom shrinks the subject and leaves dead space. [S19] (lore)
- **Give look room: the subject faces into the open side of the frame, toward the next element or the rival** — because a face pointing at the frame edge makes the composition feel cramped and cuts the line of attention. [S20] (lore)
- **In 9:16, keep the subject in the central column but offset vertically; leave the caption band and UI zones clear** — because vertical frames favour a centre bias, and platform overlays cover the edges (see 03 §9). [S21] (lore)
- **Never crop a cutout at a joint (neck, wrist, knee, ankle)** — because joint crops read as amputation; crop between joints. [S22] (lore)
- **Give each frame one focal point and lower the contrast of everything else** — because the eye goes to the point of greatest contrast first. [S7] (lore)

## 5. Text over images

- **Put any text on a plate or a gradient scrim, never raw on a busy photo, and aim for at least 4.5:1 contrast** — because WCAG's body-text minimum is 4.5:1 and a scrim creates a safe zone without hiding the image. [S23][S24] (evidence for the ratio)
- **Check legibility by eye on the busiest moment of the move** — because a passing contrast ratio does not guarantee legibility over texture or motion. [S25] (evidence: W3C working-group discussion)
- **Never set text on text: no label over a newspaper's, letter's or screen's own text** — because two text layers compete and neither is readable; move the label into clear space or offset the document. [S24][S25] (lore)
- **Don't restate what a document visibly says: either let the viewer read it (highlight, underline, zoom) or add what it doesn't say (date, name, figure)** — because the redundancy principle shows duplicate printed text on top of narration and visuals hurts uptake. [S26] (evidence)
- **Place a label next to what it names, and keep at most one label per frame beyond captions** — because spatial contiguity speeds matching, and extra elements cost attention (coherence principle). [S26] (evidence)
- **Bring elements in when the narration names them** — because temporal contiguity and signalling help viewers link word to image. [S26] (evidence)

## 6. Variety, transitions and pace of visual change

- **Assign each frame a staging type from a menu (portrait study, tableau/scene, document evidence, map/space, whiteboard or drawn diagram, number/data, object close-up, collage/montage, split/comic panel) and don't repeat a type back-to-back** — because contrast between adjacent frames raises intensity and kills the slideshow feel. [S7][owner critique] (proposal)
- **Let no single staging type exceed about a third of a reel's frames** — because a dominant type becomes the template the owner complains about. [S7][owner critique] (proposal)
- **Alternate shot scale between adjacent frames, and never cut between two near-identical framings of the same subject** — because same-scale cuts on one subject read as jump cuts. [S5] (lore)
- **Change the backdrop at least every two frames, and never use a plain black void as a default backdrop** — because tonal and colour contrast between frames carries visual progression; the void signals "nothing was designed here". [S7][owner critique] (proposal)
- **Make each frame evolve inside itself (an element enters, a mark draws, a figure turns, a second figure arrives) rather than rely on camera drift alone** — because Poynter's guidance is that motion on a still must pursue meaning, and in-frame change counts as visual change (see 03 §6). [S27] (lore)
- **Motivate every transition by a new thought, an action, a matching shape or a sound; otherwise use a hard cut** — because unmotivated effects draw attention to the edit, not the story. [S28] (lore)
- **Use transitions that carry meaning: a match cut for a time jump, a push through an object to go "inside", a page slide between documents** — because comics theory shows the kind of transition tells the viewer what changed (moment, action, subject, scene, aspect). [S8] (lore)
- **Use split or panel layouts to show two sides or simultaneous events** — because the vertical frame stacks two panels naturally and comparison is instant. [S8][S29] (lore)
- **Build complexity toward the climax, then simplify the frame at the emotional peak** — because Block's progression principle ties rising contrast to rising intensity, and a stripped frame lands the peak (see 03 "slow when intense"). [S7] (lore)

## Anti-patterns (don't)

- Don't chain "photo + Ken Burns + label" frames; a reel of single-photo frames is a slideshow.
- Don't put the same photo on the same backdrop twice, and don't default to a black void.
- Don't float a cutout centred on a flat colour with no ground, shadow or setting.
- Don't let cutouts look pasted: hard white edges, mismatched grain, light from different sides.
- Don't flip a person's screen side between consecutive frames of the same confrontation.
- Don't put a label over a document's own text, or a label that repeats it.
- Don't run captions, a lower third and a document headline all at once.
- Don't use spins, cube wipes, glitches or zooms with no motivation.
- Don't parallax every frame.
- Don't stage an imagined scene so it could pass as a real photo; stylise it as illustration.
- Don't crop faces at the chin under the UI, or crop figures at the joints.

## Where the field disagrees

- **Parallax:** a premium-doc staple to some, a cliché to others. Default: sparingly. [S14][S17]
- **Centre vs thirds in 9:16:** vertical guides split. Default: centred column, vertical offset. [S21]
