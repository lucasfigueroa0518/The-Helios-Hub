# 03 — Short-form engagement, editing and sound (the editor's and sound designer's brain)

Desk research, 2026-10-28. Scope: 60-second vertical mini-documentaries (Reels, TikTok, Shorts) told by a narrator over real stills, documents, maps and code-built elements.

**How to read a line:** `Do X — because Y. [source] [evidence | lore] (roles)`
- **evidence** = a peer-reviewed study, a platform's own statement or documentation, or a large dataset analysis. Platform-run studies are flagged "(platform-run)".
- **lore** = practitioner or creator craft, industry convention, or untested heuristics. Lore can be good advice, but nobody has measured it.
- "(unverified)" = could not confirm against a primary source.
- `[S#]` maps to the numbered source list at the bottom. `[creator practice]` = common craft with no source found.

## 1. How the platforms measure and reward

- **Treat watch time as the master metric on all three platforms** — because Instagram's head lists average watch time first among the signals to watch, TikTok calls finishing a longer video a strong interest signal, and YouTube widens a Short only if its seed audience watches instead of swiping. [S1][S10][S18] [evidence] (roles: director | editor | writer | checks)
- **Design every episode to be sent to a friend, not just liked** — because Mosseri says sends per reach weigh slightly more for reaching non-followers, while likes weigh more with existing followers. [S1] [evidence] (roles: writer | director)
- **Treat the first 3 seconds as a separate pass/fail test** — because Instagram's official tip is to "hook viewers in the first 3 seconds", Reels insights now report a skip rate for exits in that window, and YouTube Studio reports viewed vs. swiped away. [S2][S5][S18] [evidence] (roles: director | editor | writer | checks)
- **State what the story is about by second 3 and land the full hook by second 6** — because TikTok's creative guidance says exactly this, and TikTok's own ad research credits the first 6 seconds with about 90% of ad-recall impact (platform-run, ad context). [S11][S13] [evidence] (roles: writer | director)
- **Judge an episode on engaged views, average % viewed and the retention curve, not the public view count** — because YouTube (since 31 Mar 2025) and Instagram count every start and replay as a view, while YouTube's engaged views exclude loops and quick swipes. [S4][S17][S19] [evidence] (roles: checks)
- **Publish native, original files: no other platform's watermark, no borders, no reuploads** — because Instagram says watermarked, blurry, reposted or aggregated Reels are less likely to be recommended, and in April 2026 it extended no-recommendation penalties to aggregator accounts platform-wide. [S2][S3][S6][S7] [evidence] (roles: editor | checks)
- **Export full-screen 9:16 at 1080×1920 (never below 720p), under 3 minutes for Reels** — because Instagram lists non-vertical or bordered video and Reels over 3 minutes as recommendation blockers, and TikTok asks for 9:16 at 720p or better. [S2][S11] [evidence] (roles: editor | checks)
- **Never cover most of the frame with text** — because Instagram lists text covering much of the screen among the format issues that can block recommendation. [S2] [evidence] (roles: editor | checks)
- **Don't assume followers will carry an episode** — because TikTok says follower count is not a direct recommendation factor, and YouTube tests each Short on a small seed audience first. [S10][S18] [evidence] (roles: writer | director)
- **Let the story set the length, then check the platform floors** — because YouTube says no length is best for everyone and Shorts can run to 3 minutes since Oct 2024, while TikTok's Creator Rewards reportedly pays only on videos over 1 minute (unverified against current terms). [S18][S21][S22] [evidence] (roles: director | checks)

## 2. First frame and hook

- **Open on the most arresting real artifact, already in motion** — because the onset of motion and looming motion capture attention automatically, while a static image does not. [S48][S49] [evidence] (roles: director | editor)
- **Put a human face on screen in the first frame when the story has one** — because faces win the first eye movement in eye-tracking tasks, and TikTok's creator-ad report says a person in the first two seconds lifted "hooking power" 50% (platform-run). [S51][S12] [evidence] (roles: director)
- **Start in the middle of the event: no logo, title card, channel intro or greeting** — because the stay-or-swipe decision happens within about a second, and filmmakers of a serial TikTok found eye-grabbing first frames decided it. [S40][S14] [lore] (roles: director | editor | writer)
- **Pose the story's dramatic question aloud within the first 1–2 seconds** — because the makers of a 31-part TikTok serial found a striking image plus a spoken dramatic question mattered more than the cliffhanger. [S40] [lore] (roles: writer | voice)
- **Make the first frame understandable with the sound off** — because 69% of surveyed US consumers watch video with the sound off in public. [S55] [evidence] (roles: director | editor | checks)
- **Open with your fastest-moving material** — because Vox's Joss Fong says you always want "something very fast-paced at the very top". [S66] [lore] (roles: editor | director)
- **Delay the headline image until the viewer knows why it matters** — because Fong held back a key image so viewers first grasped the difficulty, and staged reveals beat all-at-once delivery in experiments. [S66][S26] [evidence] (roles: writer | director)
- **Promise only what the episode pays off** — because Instagram won't recommend clickbait or engagement bait to non-followers; that an unpaid hook teaches viewers to swipe next time is lore. [S8] [evidence] (roles: writer | checks)

## 3. Curiosity and suspense

- **Open an information gap the viewer can name in one sentence** — because curiosity is a felt deprivation that arises from a perceived gap in knowledge; a vague mystery doesn't create one. [S23] [evidence] (roles: writer)
- **Give just enough context that the viewer can almost guess the answer** — because curiosity rises with partial knowledge and confidence short of certainty, and answers to high-curiosity questions were remembered better two weeks later. [S23][S24] [evidence] (roles: writer)
- **Release information in stages: gap, small answer, bigger gap** — because a "teasing" structure (a brief gap, then resolution) produced better experiences than full information up front, though people say they prefer it all at once. [S26] [evidence] (roles: writer | editor)
- **Use the curiosity window to deliver necessary context** — because people in high-curiosity states remembered even incidental information better. [S25] [evidence] (roles: writer | director)
- **Land a reveal or turn roughly every 10–15 seconds** — because 60 seconds holds about 4–6 beats and each must renew the gap; the spacing is creator practice, not measured. [S26] [lore] (roles: writer | editor)
- **Make the open question resolvable, not confusing** — because people act to resolve a clear uncertainty even at a cost to themselves (the Pandora effect); confusion gives them nothing to resolve. [S27] [evidence] (roles: writer | checks)
- **Don't lean on "the Zeigarnik effect" as a memory hack** — because a 2025 meta-analysis found no general memory advantage for unfinished tasks; what holds up is the urge to resume them (Ovsiankina), which is the real engine of "part 2". [S28] [evidence] (roles: writer)
- **Use surprise, not just novelty** — because on TV footage about 72% of gaze shifts went to locations more surprising than average, meaning belief-changing, not merely rare. [S50] [evidence] (roles: director | editor | writer)

## 4. Emotion, sharing and comments

- **Pitch each story's emotion at high arousal: awe, indignation at a wrong, anxiety about the stakes** — because high-arousal emotions drove sharing in New York Times most-emailed data and in experiments, while low-arousal sadness reduced it. [S29] [evidence] (roles: writer | director | sound)
- **Prefer awe and wonder where the facts allow** — because at equal arousal, positive content was more viral than negative in the same study. [S29] [evidence] (roles: writer)
- **Give viewers social currency: a fact that makes the sender look in the know** — because Berger's STEPPS framework names social currency and practical value as core drivers of sharing. [S30] [evidence] (roles: writer)
- **Write one compact, quotable line the viewer can repeat** — because ideas travel inside a portable story (STEPPS "Stories"); placing it near the end is creator practice. [S30] [lore] (roles: writer | voice)
- **Know that negative words buy clicks, not shares** — because in about 105,000 headline tests each negative word lifted clicks about 2.3%, driven by sadness rather than anger, while sadness reduces sharing. [S31][S29] [evidence] (roles: writer | checks)
- **Don't build reach on us-vs-them language** — because out-group terms raised share odds about 67% per term and moral-emotional words about 12–20% per word, but the moral-emotion effect shrank on replication, and both polarize rather than inform. [S32][S33][S34] [evidence] (roles: writer | checks)
- **Ask one genuine, specific question to invite comments** — because questions in brand posts raised comment counts, while telling people to reply with set words counts as bait that platforms demote. [S35][S9] [evidence] (roles: writer)

## 5. Narrator trust and delivery

- **Keep one narrator voice, persona and sign-off across the series** — because parasocial "intimacy at a distance" grows through repeated exposure, and parasocial bonds predicted perceived credibility in influencer research. [S36][S37] [evidence] (roles: voice | director)
- **Speak to "you", the single viewer** — because TikTok's creator-ad report found saying "you" in the first 5 seconds lifted purchase intent 128% (platform-run, ad context). [S12] [evidence] (roles: writer | voice)
- **Write narration simpler than print prose** — because viewers can't re-read; Fong warns against charging ahead while the viewer is lost, and says scripts need a fresh-eyes read. [S66] [lore] (roles: writer | checks)
- **Vary pitch range from sentence to sentence** — because audio messages with pitch-range variation beat monotone ones on attention and recall. [S61] [evidence] (roles: voice | checks)
- **Don't stress every sentence** — because constant emphatic accents in radio newsreading risk hurting comprehension and attention. [S62] [evidence] (roles: voice)
- **Run a brisk baseline of about 150–170 wpm (about 150–170 words per 60 s)** — because about 150 wpm is typical conversation, comprehension holds to roughly 270 wpm, and faster speech read as more credible in a classic study; the 150–170 band for dramatic shorts is creator practice. [S74][S73][S71] [lore] (roles: writer | voice)
- **Put short silent pauses at phrase boundaries, the longest one before a reveal** — because pauses at linguistic boundaries brought recall of fast speech close to normal-rate levels and lowered listening effort. [S72] [evidence] (roles: voice | editor)
- **Slow down on the single fact the story hinges on** — because comprehension failures come from time pressure, so the key name or number needs room. [S73][S72] [lore] (roles: voice | writer)

## 6. Edit rhythm and visual change

- **Change something on screen often, and vary the interval** — because cuts trigger orienting responses, faster edit rates raised arousal and memory in the lab, and modern film shot lengths vary in a 1/f-like pattern rather than ticking evenly. [S42][S43][S45] [evidence] (roles: editor | director)
- **Treat "a new visual every 2–3 seconds" as a starting heuristic, not a law** — because the only quantified support is an unverified vendor analysis, while a peer-reviewed TikTok study found an inverted U for shot count. [S47][S46] [lore] (roles: editor | checks)
- **Slow the visual rate when the content is already intense** — because fast pacing combined with arousing content overloaded viewers and reduced recognition and recall. [S44][S41] [evidence] (roles: editor | director)
- **Cut to related images that continue the thought** — because related cuts needed less processing capacity and produced better memory than unrelated cuts. [S42] [evidence] (roles: editor)
- **Count moves, zooms, markups and new layers as "changes", not only hard cuts** — because motion onsets and surprising changes draw the eye just as cuts do. [S48][S50] [evidence] (roles: editor)
- **Cut on the voice: land picture changes on the stressed word or the start of a new thought** — because rhythm is one of Murch's core cutting criteria and the narration sets the rhythm here; untested for shorts. [S70] [lore] (roles: editor | voice)
- **Use J-cuts (next sound before next picture) to pull viewers across scene changes, and L-cuts to let a line land** — because overlapping sound hides the seam between unrelated stills. [creator practice] [lore] (roles: editor | sound)
- **When cut options conflict, protect emotion first, then story, then rhythm, then eye-trace** — because Walter Murch's Rule of Six ranks them in that order. [S70] [lore] (roles: editor | director)
- **Place the next shot's focal point where the eye already is** — because eye-trace continuity is Murch's fourth criterion and keeps fast cuts readable on a small screen. [S70] [lore] (roles: editor | director)
- **Use a match cut (same shape, position or motion) to jump between places or times** — because it carries the eye across a big story jump without losing the viewer. [creator practice] [lore] (roles: editor | director)

## 7. Stills, documents, maps and texture

- **Never hold a still without motion; move toward the detail the narration names** — because Ken Burns describes his method as "willing these still photographs alive", and motion onset captures attention. [S69][S48] [lore] (roles: editor | director)
- **Start each move with the cut, not a beat later** — because it is the onset of motion, not motion itself, that captures attention. [S48] [evidence] (roles: editor)
- **Push in for tension and discovery; pull out for context or release** — because looming motion captured attention while receding motion often did not (the finding is disputed). [S49] [evidence] (roles: editor | director)
- **Point at the evidence with markups: circles, underlines, arrows, highlights** — because signaling cues that highlight essential material improve understanding. [S53] [evidence] (roles: editor | director)
- **Separate cutouts into layers for parallax on key images only** — because depth adds life to a flat artifact, but used on every frame it makes everything float. [creator practice] [lore] (roles: editor)
- **Keep maps clean: strip labels and borders you don't need, hold the camera on each stop, scale labels with the map** — because Johnny Harris describes this as his map method. [S68] [lore] (roles: editor | director)
- **Make abstract claims concrete with an image of the physical thing** — because Fong built visuals of how an instrument is physically made precisely because the idea was abstract. [S66] [lore] (roles: director | writer)
- **Keep grain and noise subtle and fairly coarse** — because random grain is hard to compress and platforms re-encode uploads at low bitrates, so heavy fine grain turns blocky (inferred from streaming-codec work). [S78] [lore] (roles: editor | checks)
- **Make real artifacts and built reconstructions clearly different** — because the narrator's credibility is the format's main asset, and a recreated document must read as a recreation. [S37] [lore] (roles: director | checks)

## 8. Captions and on-screen text

- **Burn captions into every spoken line** — because 80% of surveyed viewers said captions make them more likely to finish a video, and Facebook's tests found captions lifted view time about 12%. [S55][S56] [evidence] (roles: editor | checks)
- **Break captions at phrase boundaries, one idea per card** — because broadcast subtitle standards break lines at natural grammar so each line is understood before it leaves. [S57] [lore] (roles: editor)
- **Keep caption pace near spoken pace, under about 180 wpm** — because BBC guidance targets about 160–180 wpm and reports that above 200 wpm many viewers can't follow (via secondary summaries). [S57] [lore] (roles: editor | checks)
- **Cap other burned-in text at about 5–10 words per second** — because TikTok's creative guidance gives that range. [S11] [evidence] (roles: editor)
- **Highlight one keyword per line, not every word in sequence** — because time-synced word highlighting was rated useful for learning but too distracting for everyday viewing. [S54] [evidence] (roles: editor)
- **Keep extra on-screen text short and next to what it labels** — because Mayer's redundancy research finds duplicate on-screen text can hurt understanding, except short text placed beside the relevant graphic. [S53] [evidence] (roles: editor | director)
- **Set captions large and high-contrast, with a stroke or plate, in one fixed band** — because TikTok creators call captions crucial for clarity, and a fixed position stops the eye from hunting over busy stills. [S14] [lore] (roles: editor | checks)

## 9. Vertical framing and safe zones

- **Keep captions, faces and key evidence out of the top ~14%, the bottom ~35% and the right-hand button column, so the key image sits in the center column** — because Meta's Reels ad guidance (as reported) reserves 14% top, 35% bottom and 6% sides, and measured Shorts ad previews show about 20% bottom and 19% right covered; organic overlays are unpublished. [S58][S59] [evidence] (roles: director | editor | checks)
- **Check each export against all three platforms' overlays before publishing** — because UI layers differ by platform and change over time. [S59] [lore] (roles: checks)

## 10. Music and tension

- **Always score with sound, never silence throughout** — because music and sound effects raised suspense and immersion over a no-audio version, and full sound design worked best. [S63] [evidence] (roles: sound)
- **Build tension with low drones, short repeated figures (ostinato, tremolo) and harmony that won't resolve** — because film-music analysis identifies these as the core tension tools. [S65] [lore] (roles: sound)
- **Escalate by adding layers, not by turning up the volume** — because the classic method adds instruments gradually to a dissonant chord. [S65] [lore] (roles: sound)
- **Use a sudden silence as a beat just before a reveal** — because dropping the music heightens tension and makes the next sound hit harder. [S65] [lore] (roles: sound | editor)
- **Change, stop or restart the music to mark each act** — because Fong changes the track about every 20 seconds and uses song endings to cue emotional beats. [S66] [lore] (roles: sound | editor)
- **Alternate neutral "thinking" music under facts with "feeling" music on earned emotional beats** — because Harris sorts his music this way and says emotional music used too early "cheapens your video". [S67] [lore] (roles: sound | writer)
- **Pick music that never competes with the narration: no lead vocals or busy melodies under speech** — because Fong rejects tracks that pull attention from what is being said. [S66] [lore] (roles: sound)
- **Land act turns on a musical downbeat, not every cut** — because syncing big changes to the music's structure makes turns feel deliberate, while cutting on every beat is music-video grammar. [creator practice] [lore] (roles: editor | sound)

## 11. Sound design and foley

- **Sync a sound to every key visual event: stamp, pin, highlight, reveal** — because a tone synchronized with a visual change makes it "pop" and draws attention automatically, even when the sound carries no location information. [S52] [evidence] (roles: sound | editor)
- **Use descriptive sound effects to turn stills into places** — because sound effects and close "sound shots" raised listeners' mental imagery, which tracked their attention. [S60] [evidence] (roles: sound)
- **Make foley literal to the artifact: paper slide, pin push, marker squeak, shutter, typewriter, cord twang** — because purposeful foley tells the viewer what object they're looking at; generic whooshes don't. [S60] [lore] (roles: sound)
- **Mix for phone speakers** — because most viewing is on phones: give drones audible upper harmonics and check narration intelligibility on a phone. [creator practice] [lore] (roles: sound | checks)

## 12. Mix and loudness

- **Keep narration the loudest, steadiest layer; duck music about 10–20 dB under it and ride the duck** — because tutorials cluster around 6–20 dB with no formal standard, and a duck that slams on every line becomes audible. [S77] [lore] (roles: sound)
- **Master to about −14 LUFS integrated, with true peak at or below −1 dBTP** — because YouTube is widely reported to turn louder audio down to about −14 LUFS without raising quiet audio, while TikTok and Instagram publish no target (unverified). [S76][S75] [lore] (roles: sound | checks)

## 13. Endings, loops and series

- **End on the next unanswered question, not a summary** — because cliffhanger endings increased audiences' desire for the next installment without lowering enjoyment. [S39] [evidence] (roles: writer)
- **Make each part satisfying on its own, with one thread left open** — because a TV lab study found cliffhangers raised arousal but not intention to continue, so the episode itself has to deliver. [S38][S40] [evidence] (roles: writer | director)
- **Name the next part and what it will reveal** — because the 31-part TikTok serial ended each episode with a follow prompt for the next one, and its makers recut episodes to raise follows. [S40] [lore] (roles: writer | voice)
- **Write the last line and frame to flow back into the first** — because replays count as views on YouTube and Instagram; that seamless loops drive rewatches is creator lore. [S19][S4] [lore] (roles: writer | editor)
- **Open each follow-up part with a recap that also works as a hook** — because many viewers arrive mid-series from the feed. [creator practice] [lore] (roles: writer | editor)
- **Bridge to the next part with a platform link or a pinned comment** — because YouTube lets a Short link to another Short or video (conversion is reportedly low). [S20] [evidence] (roles: checks)

## Anti-patterns (don't)

- **Don't open on a logo, title card, slow fade from black, ambient intro or "hey guys".** It spends the 3-second test on nothing. [S2][S40]
- **Don't hold a still with no move, markup or sound.** A frozen frame gives the eye no onset to follow. [S48]
- **Don't cut fast and dramatize hard at the same time.** Fast pacing plus arousing content overloads memory. [S44]
- **Don't put captions or evidence under the platform UI**, and don't cover faces or the key detail with captions. [S58][S59]
- **Don't fill the frame with text or animate every caption word.** Both can block recommendation or distract. [S2][S54]
- **Don't use engagement bait** ("comment YES", "like if…", "tag someone"). Platforms demote it. [S8][S9]
- **Don't write a hook the episode can't pay off, or a fake cliffhanger.** It is clickbait under Instagram's guidelines and burns trust. [S8]
- **Don't cross-post files carrying another platform's watermark, and don't delete and re-upload weak Shorts.** [S2][S18]
- **Don't farm outrage or us-vs-them framing for reach.** It polarizes and erodes the narrator's credibility. [S33][S34]
- **Don't put emotional music in before the story has earned it**, and don't run one track wall-to-wall with no act change. [S66][S67]
- **Don't put lead vocals or busy melodies under narration.** [S66]
- **Don't add whooshes, risers or glitch transitions with no on-screen cause, and don't use speed ramps as filler.** They blunt the sync cue that makes real events pop. [S52]
- **Don't read flat, and don't stress every word.** [S61][S62]
- **Don't end on a summary** ("so that's the story of…"). End on the next question. [S39]
- **Don't add heavy fine grain or noise.** The platform re-encode turns it to mush. [S78]
- **Don't present a reconstruction as a real document.** [S37]

## Where the field disagrees

- **Cut rate.** Faster edits raised arousal and memory in one lab study [S43], but fast pacing plus arousing content caused overload in another [S44]. A TikTok study found an inverted U for shot count [S46], and the popular "every 2–3 s" rule rests on unverified vendor data [S47]. Working position: frequent change, varied intervals, slower on intense beats.
- **Sound-off vs. sound-on.** 69% of surveyed US viewers watch sound-off in public [S55], yet Meta says over 80% of daily Reels plays are sound-on [S16], and 88% of TikTok users told Kantar that sound is essential [S15]. Both claims are self-interested. Working position: the picture and captions must work silent, and the sound must reward people who listen.
- **Captions vs. the redundancy principle.** Captions lift completion and view time [S55][S56], but Mayer finds narration plus identical on-screen text can hurt learning [S53], and word-synced highlighting distracted everyday viewers [S54]. No study tests word-by-word pop captions in short-form.
- **Cliffhangers.** One TV lab experiment found cliffhangers raised arousal but not intention to continue or enjoyment [S38]. Text-narrative experiments found more desire for the next installment [S39]. Practitioners say the hook matters more than the cliffhanger [S40].
- **Negativity.** Negative headline words raise clicks, with sadness driving the effect [S31], yet sadness lowers sharing and anger raises it [S29]. Clicks and shares are different jobs.
- **Zeigarnik.** Creator lore says unfinished things are better remembered. A 2025 meta-analysis says no; the urge to resume (Ovsiankina) is what holds [S28].
- **Loudness.** −14 LUFS is community consensus for YouTube [S76]. AES TD1008 sets a lower speech target (about −18 LUFS, per a secondary summary) [S75]. Ian Shepherd calls −14 a reference, not a target (reported). TikTok and Instagram targets are unpublished.
- **Speech rate.** Fast speech was more persuasive and credible in 1976 [S71]. Later work is mixed: speed can cut message processing, and slow speech persuades the already-converted (secondary summary) [S71].

## Gaps

- No primary interviews found on the edit craft of MagnatesMedia, ColdFusion or true-crime TikTok channels. Any claim about their pacing, texture or stills treatment is unverified, so none is used above.
- No official loudness target published for TikTok or Instagram Reels, and no official organic safe-zone spec for any of the three platforms.
- No study tests word-by-word animated captions, speed ramps, J/L cuts or match cuts in short-form.
- No measured WPM norm for dramatic short-form narration. The 150–170 wpm band is lore.
- Thayer & Levenson (1983) tested scores meant to raise or lower stress responses to one film [S64]. Its results could not be confirmed here, so it is listed but not relied on.
- The web-search budget ran out before every DOI could be confirmed. The Rathje et al. entry links an index listing, not the journal page.
