# Story calibration — what a good Reel AI Drama story is (Lucas, 2026-10-28)

Lucas's list (developed with a Claude chat) of stories worth producing as docs. It **calibrates** discovery: the
traits below come from it. The facts in these pitches are **unverified**; reporting verifies before anything is made.

> **Prompt-pollution rule:** these stories are never pasted into a generator or writer prompt as examples
> (they would be rehashed into every pitch). Prompts get the traits, written abstractly. The stories are
> used as (1) the offline calibration set for the Jev drama and material scores, and (2) seed ideas for
> the pool if Lucas says so.

## The list (verbatim)

**Conflict**

1. **Musk vs. Altman.** Elon Musk put $38 million into OpenAI, then sued, accusing Altman and Brockman of quietly bolting a for-profit onto the charity. He wanted both removed and over $150 billion in damages. The jury took less than two hours to throw the case out as filed too late. Altman won, but several witnesses had called him a liar along the way. Musk's response: they "stole a charity," and he's appealing.
2. **Ilya's 52-page memo.** OpenAI's chief scientist spent at least a year considering firing Altman, waiting until most of the board wasn't clearly on Sam's side. His memo accused Altman of lying and turning executives against each other, and he sent it through a disappearing email so Altman couldn't make it vanish. The twist: most of the evidence came from CTO Mira Murati. Within 24 hours of the firing, the Amodei siblings were on a call about Anthropic taking over OpenAI. Altman was back in five days.
3. **Zuckerberg's $100 million texts.** After Meta's model flopped, Zuckerberg started personally WhatsApping hundreds of researchers, hosting dinners at his Palo Alto and Lake Tahoe homes, and coordinating targets in a chat called "Recruiting Party 🎉." Some recipients thought the messages were fake. Altman called the $100 million offers "crazy," then lost three researchers to Meta days later.
4. **The godfather vs. the 28-year-old.** Turing Award winner Yann LeCun ran Meta's AI lab for 12 years, until Zuckerberg spent over $14 billion on Scale AI and made LeCun report to its 28-year-old founder, Alexandr Wang. LeCun had long doubted that chatbots lead to real intelligence, the exact bet Zuckerberg was making. He quit to start his own company.
5. **Murati vs. OpenAI, round two.** Barret Zoph co-founded Thinking Machines with Murati. He told her he was thinking of leaving; days later, she fired him for "unethical conduct." 58 minutes after her announcement, OpenAI announced it was hiring him back and said it didn't share her concerns. This is the same Murati who supplied the evidence to oust Altman.

**Positive**

6. **The Nobel call that looked like spam.** Demis Hassabis and John Jumper won the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for AlphaFold, which predicted the structure of nearly all 200 million known proteins. Jumper almost dismissed the Swedish number, hoping it wasn't just a delivery notice. DeepMind made the database free, and over two million researchers in 190 countries have used it.
7. **From Denny's dishwasher to Nvidia.** Jensen Huang moved to the U.S. from Taiwan at 9 and started at Denny's at 15, as a dishwasher, busboy and waiter. In 1993, he and two friends sketched out Nvidia in a Denny's booth in San Jose. That booth now has a plaque calling it the booth that launched a $1 trillion company.

**Founding decisions**

8. **The "low-key research preview."** OpenAI staff ran a betting pool on ChatGPT's first-week users; the top guess was 100,000. The launch was so quiet that some employees didn't know it happened. It hit a million users in five days and about 100 million in two months. "Low-key research preview" became a joke employees put on laptop stickers.
9. **Google's eight inventors.** In 2017, eight Google researchers wrote "Attention Is All You Need," the paper behind ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini. One by one, every author left. Google later paid $2.7 billion to bring Noam Shazeer back; this year he left for OpenAI. Zero of the eight remain.
10. **Nvidia's 30 days.** Nvidia's first chip bet on the wrong graphics technology, and a follow-up chip for Sega was canceled. With 30 days of cash left, Huang flew to Japan, admitted the failure to Sega's CEO Shoichiro Irimajiri, and asked him to invest $5 million anyway, warning it would most likely be lost. Irimajiri said yes. Nvidia survived, and the investment turned out to be his best decision as Sega's president. Had Sega held the stake, it would be worth about $1 trillion today. This July, Huang went back to Tokyo to thank him in person, 30 years later.
11. **DeepMind turns down Zuckerberg.** Facebook offered more money, but Hassabis sold to Google, saying he wanted a buyer who understood why AI would be bigger than everything else. The condition: no use of DeepMind's work for military weapons or surveillance, overseen by an ethics board. A former employee later said that board never met. This month, DeepMind researchers resigned over Google's military contracts.
12. **The Rosewood dinner.** In the summer of 2015, Elon Musk and Sam Altman gathered a small group at the Rosewood hotel on Sand Hill Road to discuss starting an AI lab, with Stripe CTO Greg Brockman, Google Brain's Ilya Sutskever, and Dario Amodei, then also at Google. Musk was the draw: Sutskever, who had been cold-emailed, accepted eagerly once he heard Musk would be there. Brockman and Sutskever met for the first time that night and went on to co-found OpenAI. The kicker is what became of the table: Musk sued Altman, Sutskever tried to fire him, and Amodei left to found Anthropic, OpenAI's biggest rival. Even the founding myth is disputed; Altman says there were around 20 "founding dinners" that year, and this one just became canon.

## Lucas's corrections (2026-10-28) — supersede my first reading below

- **What we look for (search criteria):**
  1. **People of the AI world with wants, in situations that matter.** Not only conflicts and underdogs:
     "I want to consider other types of stories that also revolve around the way that the AI world was built."
  2. **A vivid, concrete detail or moment that makes it a scene.** ("pretty good")
  3. **An implication for the AI world.** All stories are in the same universe; each must be "meaningful
     enough that they have an implication on the AI world."
- **Not search criteria, just qualities of good stories:** the stunning number, the ironic kicker, small acts
  with big consequences (my traits 3–5). They belong to the telling, not the search.
- **Not a trait:** the ~100-word length (Lucas asked the chat to tighten them).

## Traits these share (my first reading; corrected above)

Drama:
1. **Famous people with wants, in collision.** Every story turns on named, recognizable figures. Most are
   two figures in conflict, or one figure against long odds.
2. **One vivid, physical detail that makes it a scene:** a 52-page memo sent by disappearing email, a group
   chat called "Recruiting Party 🎉", a Denny's booth with a plaque, a Nobel call mistaken for a delivery
   notice, laptop stickers.
3. **A number that stuns, often a small one against a huge one:** under two hours vs $150 billion,
   58 minutes, 30 days of cash, $5 million vs $1 trillion, zero of eight.
4. **A twist or ironic kicker that recontextualizes everything:** the evidence came from Murati; the ethics
   board never met; the dinner table became enemies; the founding myth is disputed.
5. **Small acts with outsized consequences:** a dinner, a memo, a cold call, a quiet launch, a loan.
6. **A shared universe.** The same characters recur across stories (Altman, Musk, Murati, Sutskever,
   Amodei, Hassabis, Zuckerberg), so each story sits inside a bigger saga (D-6, Lucas's version).
7. **Pitchable in about 100 words** with a beginning, a turn and a kicker.

Time: most are **history or backstory**, not breaking news (2015, 1993, 2017, 2022, 2024). Recency
helps when there is a fresh development (a verdict, a resignation "this month"), often as the reason to
tell an older story now.

Material (what makes images findable):
- **Heavily photographed people**: blue-chip figures with years of public photos.
- **Real, nameable places**: a hotel, a diner booth, a lakeside home, a courtroom, Stockholm, Tokyo.
- **Published artifacts**: a paper's first page, filings, posts and statements with timestamps, a memo
  reported in the press, a plaque, a chart of user growth.
- **Photographed events**: trials, award ceremonies, keynotes, launches.

Lanes Lucas labelled: **Conflict**, **Positive**, **Founding decisions**.
