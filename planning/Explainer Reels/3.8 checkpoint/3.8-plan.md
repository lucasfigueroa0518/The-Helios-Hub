## Part A: the analogy writer (`lib/explainers/ideas/generator.ts`, analogy-writer-v7)

### A1. The system prompt

**BEFORE (opening, v6):**
> You write analogies for Helios Explainer Reels: one-minute, 9:16 animated reels that each teach one computer-science, software, or AI concept to a broad audience. The analogy is the reel's visual anchor: one scene, drawn as simple paper animation, that the whole reel revolves around. The scene can be objects, a physical effect, or an everyday action by a small cartoon character called a critter: use whichever carries the mechanism most simply, and never add a critter the mechanism does not need. The reel opens on a hook moment inside this scene, asks its question inside it, plays it out step by step, maps each element onto the real component it stands for, and walks a tiny real example through it. The theme brief below defines the audience. It is binding.
>
> What to write: The simplest physical or conceptual metaphor that carries the mechanism. Start from basic elements, situations, objects that are universally understandable or part of everyday life. Use an everyday place only if it requires 3 or fewer recognizable parts for the viewer to understand the everyday place.

**AFTER (opening, v7).** This is Lucas's rewrite, with his guidance paragraph turned into direction:
> You write the central analogy for a Helios educational "brain break" reel: a one-minute, 9:16 animated reel that teaches one computer-science, software, or AI concept to a broad audience. The analogy is the reel's visual, demonstrative, educational, and explanatory anchor. The reel opens on a moment inside it, plays it out step by step, maps each part onto the real component it stands for, and then walks a tiny real example through the real thing. The theme brief below defines the audience. It is binding.
>
> What to write: the most apt and memorable physical or conceptual metaphor that carries the mechanism. Start from basic elements, situations, objects that are universally understandable or part of everyday life. Use an everyday place only if it requires 3 or fewer recognizable parts for the viewer to understand the everyday place.
>
> **What makes a great analogy.** Your goal is the strongest analogy for this concept: one a viewer enjoys, understands at once, and remembers, so the concept sticks.
> - **Apt.** The analogy and the concept are close in nature across their dimensions: the same kind of problem, parts that do the same jobs, the same reason it works, and the same way it goes wrong. Understanding the analogy should take the viewer most of the way to understanding the concept.
> - **Interesting.** It grabs people: vivid, surprising, or fun to picture, a world a viewer wants to watch for a minute. Never choose a dull comparison because it is safe, and never a clever one the viewer has to decode.
> - **Instantly understood.** The viewer already knows how everything in it behaves, so the reel uses what they know instead of teaching it.
> - **Memorable.** It is the picture a viewer would reach for to explain the concept to a friend tomorrow.
>
> **Analogies take many shapes.** Use whichever fits this concept best: an everyday object or tool; a physical or natural effect (light, water, gravity, heat, growth); an everyday action or routine; a familiar place or system whose parts already work this way (a kitchen, a post office, a library); a game or a sport; nature or the human body as a system; a small situation between characters. Objects can carry a mechanism on their own; add a character only when someone's action is part of the mechanism.
>
> **Your job in the system.** You choose the comparison and map it onto the concept. Jev scores your three candidates and code picks one. Then a director writes the reel's story, every spoken line, the opening question and the payoff, and illustrators decide how each shot is drawn, framed and kept uncluttered. Give them a strong comparison and a precise mapping. Do not write narration, shot plans, or drawing details.

**"Another adjective?"** I added "most apt and memorable" in place of "simplest". "Apt" is the word Lucas used for what matters. "Simple" now lives under "instantly understood".

## Part B: Jev's analogy scoring v6 (`lib/explainers/jev/questions/analogy-scoring.ts`)

Without this part, the prompt change is undone: today's scorer still rewards the ladder ("simplicity") and a payoff the writer no longer owns, and nothing rewards an interesting analogy.

### B1. Questions, before → after

- **`simplicity` → `aptness`.**
  - *Question:* "How close in nature are this analogy and the concept: would understanding one take a viewer most of the way to understanding the other?"
  - *Judge:* compare across the concept's dimensions: the kind of problem, what each part does, why it works, its scale and limits, and how it goes wrong. Reward deep kinship. Penalize a match on one surface feature, or a picture so stripped down it says little about the concept.
  - *Levels:* "Unrelated" up to "Twin".
- **`visual_payoff` → `interest`.**
  - *Question:* "Would a scrolling viewer find this analogy interesting enough to want to watch it play out?"
  - *Judge:* reward vivid, surprising, playful or emotionally familiar worlds a viewer enjoys picturing. Penalize dull, generic or obvious comparisons chosen for safety, and clever ones the viewer must decode.
  - *Levels:* "Dull" up to "Irresistible".
- **`visual_strength`:** the judge drops "simple cartoon critters for every character" and "visible as motion and changing state rather than explained by narration". It now reads "…simple cartoon characters…" and "…whose mechanism can be shown, not only told."
- **`reach`, `mapping_fidelity`, `familiarity`, `hook_clarity`:** unchanged. Familiarity keeps its "an invented rule scores at most 1", which is where invented rules are still caught.

### B2. Weights, floors, tie-breaks (`lib/explainers/ideas/analogy.ts`)

|---|---|---|
| Weights | 100/7 each | aptness 20 · mapping_fidelity 20 · interest 15 · familiarity 15 · hook_clarity 10 · reach 10 · visual_strength 10 |

## Part C: the curiosity gap writer becomes its own step, and Jev picks (EX3-120)

### C0. Where it runs (new, mirrors the analogy pipeline)

**Before:** the director writes three gap candidates inside Session A, rates them itself and picks one.

**After:** a **gap writer** writes 3 candidates, Jev scores them, and code picks one before the director ever starts.
- **The writer:** one Sonnet call, with the cached system prompt in C1, like the analogy writer.
- **When it runs:**
  - in the idea phase, right after the topic's analogy is picked (`lib/explainers/ideas/cycle.ts`);
  - at render, as a new stage `gap` after the `analogy` stage in `lib/explainers/render/job.ts`, when the topic's gap note is missing or from an older version.
- **What it reads:** the topic, its term, its scope and the picked analogy. It knows the analogy only so its question is one the analogy can answer in frame 4; it never uses the analogy's world.
- **What it returns:** 3 candidates as JSON, `{ line, question, way_in, why_it_pulls }`.
- **Code checks first**, before any Jev spend. These reuse the lint's helpers (`TOSS_RE`, term matching, word count). A candidate that fails any of these is out:
  - it ends on its question;
  - it has no toss;
  - it doesn't use the term;
  - it is 18–36 words;
  - it names no analogy element.
- **Jev scores** the rest (new set `explainers-gap-scoring-v1`, `lib/explainers/jev/questions/gap-scoring.ts`):
  - **pull:** would a stranger scrolling past stop, stay, or send it?
  - **clarity:** could they say the question back?
  - **closable:** does the picked analogy answer this question?
  - **accurate:** a yes/no gate; every fact, number and claim is true.
- **Code picks** the highest weighted total (pull 50 · clarity 25 · closable 25) among accurate candidates with clarity and closable at 3.5 or more. Ties go to pull.
  - None qualifies: one more pass, told what missed (like the analogy retry).
  - Still none: the render fails, saying so. The topic is not dropped, so the next try writes new gaps.
- **Storage:** `jev_notes.gap` on the topic, with its version (`lib/explainers/ideas/gap.ts`, new, mirroring analogy.ts: `chooseGap`, `pickGap`, `topicGap`, `gapMarkdown`).
- **The brief:** BRIEF.md gains `## Curiosity gap` with the pick, the question, and the other two candidates with their scores (`briefMarkdown`, `lib/explainers/render/workspace.ts`).
- **Cost:** about $0.03–0.06 per topic.

### C1. The gap writer's prompt (was story-design.md § 2's writing rules)

**BEFORE (the formula):**
> The line is one tiny real situation, in this order (18–36 words):
> 1. **What they do or see**, every day, with this technology, named as they name it (a chatbot, their phone's keyboard, a search box, a shop's website)…
> 2. **The part they can't explain**…
> 3. **Their own question**, short and natural, last…
>
> Hard rules: About the real thing… · End on the question. No toss… · No term, no jargon… · Its own picture… · The loop closes explicitly… · Write `gap_question:`…
>
> Pick the best gap… Rate each from 1 to 5 on clarity, pull, relevance and closable. Pick the strongest…

**AFTER:**
> ### 2 · Curiosity gap: the question about the real thing that a viewer can't scroll past (EX3-101, EX3-115, EX3-120)
>
> **Your goal is engagement.** Write the opening question with the most pull for this topic: the one a stranger scrolling past would most want answered, and would stay for, comment on, or send to a friend. The hook (frame 1) showed the analogy's moment; frame 2 cuts to the real technology the reel teaches and opens the gap the rest of the reel closes. A curiosity gap is never confusion: the viewer knows exactly what they are missing, and wants it.
>
> **Ways in.** Choose the one with the most pull for this topic, combine them, or invent a better one:
> - **The everyday mystery:** something they do every day that secretly works in a surprising way.
> - **The contradiction:** two true things that seem impossible together.
> - **The stakes:** what breaks, leaks or costs money when this goes wrong.
> - **The impossible-seeming number:** a speed or scale that shouldn't be possible.
> - **The belief that's wrong:** what most people assume, and the hint that it isn't so.
> - **The "you did this today":** a moment from their own day they never questioned.
>
> Examples, each a different way in:
> - "You type half a word and your phone already guesses the rest. So how does it know what you meant to say?" (everyday mystery)
> - "A chatbot can pass a bar exam, then miscount the r's in 'strawberry'. How can both be true?" (contradiction)
> - "Every app hides a secret key. Leak it, and a stranger can run up someone else's bill. So why do apps need one at all?" (stakes)
>
> **The only hard rules:**
> - **About the real technology, never the analogy.** Nothing from the anchor scene appears in it, and it gets its own picture (`anchor: own`).
> - **True.** Every fact, number and claim is accurate; never invent a statistic or an anecdote.
> - **End on the question. No toss** (EX3-116): nothing after it.
> - **No term, no jargon.** The title card and frame 3 name it.
> - **18–36 words** (frame 2 is 9 seconds).
> - **The loop closes.** Frame 4's last sentence answers it, and frame 6's real example shows the case. Write `gap_question:`.
>
> **What to return.** Exactly 3 candidates, each using a different way in, as JSON matching the schema: the line, its question, the way in, and one line on why it pulls. Jev scores them on pull, clarity, whether the analogy closes it, and accuracy, and the strongest is picked. Do not write narration for any other part of the reel.

(The director's own picture, `anchor: own`, and the loop closing in frame 4 stay with the director; see C2.)

**What goes:**

- the "relevance" rating, which becomes one way in ("you did this today") instead of a requirement.

**What stays:**
