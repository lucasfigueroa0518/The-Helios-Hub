The bottom line

The system is built to make reels that follow the rules, not reels that teach. Each part is written by a separate writer and scored on its own terms: the topic, the analogy, the gap, the script and every frame. Nobody owns the explanation as a whole.

The one check that measures understanding has failed on every recent reel, and it cannot stop a reel from being built.

The basic idea does land: in every test, the stranger could say back what the concept is. What fails is the part that makes a viewer feel enlightened:

  • the step that explains why it works;
  • knowing what stands for what while the analogy plays;
  • seeing the pattern again in the real example;
  • an ending that answers the opening question and leaves one clear takeaway.
These are design and structure problems. More rules won't fix them: the system already has more than 120 numbered decisions, and the rules collide.

What a reel that teaches needs

This is the target the gaps are measured against. It draws on the established multimedia-learning principles (Mayer: signaling, contiguity, segmenting, pre-training) and on research on learning by analogy (comparing two cases that share one structure).

1

One spine. The reel answers one question with one insight. Every beat moves toward that answer, and the answer lands as the payoff.

2

Name it while you show it. The viewer learns what each part stands for at the moment it appears, not ten seconds later. Signaling and temporal contiguity.

3

Say and show the why. The step that makes the mechanism click is spoken and visible. A list of what happens is not an explanation.

4

See the pattern twice. The real example walks the same steps as the analogy, in a different world, so the viewer makes the transfer themselves. Analogical encoding.

5

One world at a time, with bridges. Every switch between the analogy and the real thing is visible and explained.

6

Few new words, each defined. Beyond the term, at most one or two technical words, each in plain words the first time. Pre-training.

7

Room at the idea boundaries. An energetic pace is fine (180–200 wpm is inside the range people understand), but the question and the click need a short beat after them. Segmenting.

8

An earned ending. The question from the gap is answered in real terms near the end, followed by one sentence the viewer keeps.

G1

No teaching spine: every part is optimized on its own

CriticalIdea phase → director

Now

  • Four separate passes. The idea phase scores the topic. Then the analogy writer runs, scored by Jev on 7 qualities. Then the gap writer runs, scored by Jev on pull, clarity and whether the analogy answers it.
  • Locked inputs. The director gets the analogy and the gap fixed, and builds a story around them.
  • No shared target. No step ever writes down the one insight the reel exists to deliver, or the step that makes it click. Nothing scores the analogy or the gap against them.
  • A "thesis" field exists in the storyboard frontmatter. The director writes it, and no check reads it.

Why it hurts

  • The parts don't add up to one explanation. Each part is strong on its own scale.
  • Cache reel example. The gap asks why software shows out-of-date information on purpose, which is the trade-off. Frame 4 answers with speed. The trade-off appears once, in frame 5's last sentence, and never in the real example.
  • The 3.8 round's failures come from the same split. A stricter analogy bar and gap floors, with no shared target, rejected most candidates without getting closer to a better reel.

Fix

The idea phase writes a teaching spine first. One Sonnet call per topic, about $0.02, with four fields:

  • takeaway: the one sentence the viewer keeps;
  • click: the step that explains why it works;
  • misconception: what people wrongly assume;
  • limit: where it breaks, or its trade-off.

Everything else is measured against it:

  • The analogy writer gets the spine. Jev's analogy scoring adds "does the process show the click step?"
  • The gap writer gets the takeaway, and the gap's "closable" score asks whether the takeaway answers it.
  • BRIEF.md carries the spine. The director's beats are checked against it.
G2

The mapping comes after the analogy has already played

CriticalBeats 4–5Your call · beat jobs

Now

  • Frame 4 plays the scene for 12 seconds. Nothing says what the cook, the jar or the pantry stands for.
  • Frame 5 then lists "the X is the Y, because…": three or four pairs in about 10 seconds.
  • The format leaks: 26 "because" clauses across 9 reels. Most justify the label rather than explain the mechanism.

Why it hurts

  • The viewer watches the key scene without knowing what it means. Then they get a list too fast to follow by ear.
  • The test stranger flagged this in 4 of 7 full-reel tests.

Fix

  • Map as it plays. In frame 4, each element is introduced with its real name, spoken and labeled on the word: "The jar is our cache: small, and right by the stove."
  • Frame 5 changes job. It becomes "why it works": the click step from the spine, shown in the scene. Then the limit: where the comparison, or the system, breaks.
  • The rule changes: "because" must give the mechanism, not the reason for the label.
G3

The step that explains why gets cut

CriticalBeat 4 word budget

Now

  • Frame 4 has 20–36 words to do four jobs:
    • pick up the scene;
    • introduce up to four elements, each "by name and job";
    • play 3–5 steps;
    • answer the gap in its last sentence.
  • That doesn't fit. Nothing marks which step is the essential one, so the director drops whichever step costs the most words. That is usually the causal one.

Why it hurts

All 9 reels dropped the step their analogy was picked for.

  • Cache: the jar never gets refilled.
  • Model routing: it never says how the router decides.
  • RAG: it never says how the closest chunk is found.
  • Prompt injection: it never says why the AI can't tell orders from content.

Fix

  • The spine's click step is required. Frame 4 or frame 5 must say it and show it, and the editor (G16) checks that it does.
  • Rebalance the words. Move words to beats 4 and 5 from beat 7, and from the shorter definition (G7).
  • Lighten frame 4's load. Introducing elements moves into the mapping (G2), so it takes fewer words.

G8

No beat to think after the question or the click

Mediumpace.mjsYour call · EX3-79

Now

Every pause is capped at 0.2 seconds, including after the gap's question and after the line where it clicks. The reels average 180–200 words per minute.

Why it matters

  • The speed itself is fine. Comprehension holds up to about 250 words per minute (Foulke and Sticht), and fast, energetic speakers held edX viewers longest (Guo, Kim and Rubin, 2014).
  • The research supports breaks at idea boundaries, not a slower voice (segmenting). Ours has none.

Fix

  • Keep the pace and the 0.2s cap everywhere else.
  • Allow exactly two longer beats of about 0.4s: one after the gap's question, one after the click line.
  • Cut words in beats 3 and 5 rather than slowing the voice.

HighAll prompts

Now

  • Each frame worker reads about 11,000 words before any code, then kit files whose comments carry more rules. frame.md alone has 63 "never", 60 "every" and 22 "HARD CONSTRAINT".

G15

The understanding test can't tell good from bad, and can't block

CriticalSession A · job.ts:598–608Your call · EX3-108

Now

  • The pass rule can't be met. A test fails if anything at all is listed as confusing, and the stranger is told never to be polite about confusion. So every test fails: 14 of 14 on the 7 newest reels, both tries each.
  • Nothing happens on a fail. The worker logs it and builds anyway ("Recorded, never blocking").
  • The director grades itself. It writes the verdict and explains each fail away.
  • Its notes go nowhere. Notes the director leaves for the build are never read.
  • The test is missing what viewers see. Its input leaves out the title card, so some complaints that the concept "is never named" come from the test, not the reel.
That link is the design (the analogy returns in frame 4 and closes the gap), so it is recorded only.director, cache 0a363b52, gap-check.json

Why it hurts

  • The test's own findings could have driven the fixes. It has been reporting the same structural problems for seven reels: the vague opener, the hook-to-gap jump, late mapping, undefined words, no recap.
  • No reel gets better because of the test.
  • Every test fails, so a pass/fail count can't tell a good reel from a bad one.

Fix

  • Graded severity, computed by code from the stranger's JSON:
    • Blocking: the mechanism is said back wrong, the example's answer is wrong or ambiguous, the gap's question goes unanswered, or a character seems to change.
    • Minor: a single word or a picture detail.
  • A blocking finding stops audio until one rewrite has been tried. If it still fails, the reel builds and is flagged to you.
  • Recurring confusions get reported to you. Ones that come from the fixed design are rolled up across reels into a design report, so they stop being excused one reel at a time.

G20

Visual caps and timing strip out the mechanism

Highframe.md · shots.js · picture check

Now

  • Six different limits on what can be on screen:
    • at most three readable things;
    • a medium shot shows two or three entities;
    • every shot draws every entity;
    • at most four entities;
    • at most five objects;
    • half the frame empty, while the scene must also fill the frame.
    The picture check fails a tile that names more than three things, and labels count as things.
  • "One thing to read at a time" rules out a label while captions are on, and captions are on whenever the voice speaks.
  • The orange highlight is fixed on the hero, and the focus may change only at a beat boundary. So the step being spoken can't be highlighted.
  • Cuts come every 4.5 seconds at most, on peak motion, and a frame ends 0.15s after its voice. The result of each step is barely on screen. In the cache reel, frame 4's establishing shot lasted 2 seconds.
  • The step tracker that beat 6 relies on doesn't exist in the kit.

Fix

  • One limit for what's on screen: four entities, one of them the lead, the rest visible but quiet, with clear ground around the lead.
  • Allow the caption plus one label that names what the caption is saying.
  • Separate identity from attention. The hero keeps its identity through what it wears; the orange highlight moves with the spoken step.
  • Hold the result: each step's end state stays on screen at least 0.8s. The establishing shot and the payoff shot may run up to 6s.
  • Build the missing tracker: add stepTracker() to the kit's cards, drawn outside the camera like the dictionary card.
