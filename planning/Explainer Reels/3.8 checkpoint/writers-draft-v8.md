# Draft: analogy writer v8 and gap writer v2 (before / after)

Draft only, 2026-10-25. Nothing here is in the system. Built from Lucas's reasons the 3.8 sets were horrible:
- not apt;
- doesn't map well;
- weird or too niche;
- complicated in weird ways;
- not easy to understand or relate to;
- the viewer has zero interest.

It also uses what we agreed on:
- care judged by recognition, tension inside the scene, and the "oh, that's it" moment;
- the user / builder / curiosity seat.

---

## Analogy writer

### 1. What the analogy is for

**Before (v7):**
> What to write: the most apt and memorable physical or conceptual metaphor that carries the mechanism. Start from basic elements, situations, objects that are universally understandable or part of everyday life. Use an everyday place only if it requires 3 or fewer recognizable parts for the viewer to understand the everyday place.

**After (v8):**
> What to write: a moment from ordinary life that works the same way the concept works, so that a viewer who watches it understands the concept, recognizes themselves in it, and wants to see how it turns out.

**Why:**
- "Metaphor that carries the mechanism" let the writer go anywhere: a cell's ribosome, sonar, a pirate's dial.
- "A moment from ordinary life" sets the territory. "Works the same way" sets the bar.

### 2. What makes a great analogy

**Before (v7):** four qualities, unranked.
> - Apt. The analogy and the concept are close in nature across their dimensions…
> - Interesting. It grabs people: vivid, surprising, or fun to picture, a world a viewer wants to watch for a minute…
> - Instantly understood. The viewer already knows how everything in it behaves…
> - Memorable. It is the picture a viewer would reach for to explain the concept to a friend tomorrow.

**After (v8):** four qualities, ranked, with "interesting" replaced by the two things that actually make a viewer care.
> What makes a great analogy. A viewer should think "I've been there" in the first second and "oh, so that's how it works" by the end. Four things make that happen, in this order of importance:
> - **Apt.** It works for the same reason the concept works. The part that makes the concept click happens in the analogy too, for the same reason, and each part does the same job as the real part it stands for. A comparison that only looks alike, or matches one step, is not apt.
> - **Easy.** The viewer understands it with no explanation: two to four parts, one situation, behavior everyone already knows. If it needs a sentence of setup, or a rule the viewer has to be told, it is too complicated.
> - **Lived.** It is a moment the viewer has personally been in, more than once: at home, with food, running errands, on their phone, with family or friends, at work, shopping, travelling. A place they have only heard of, a job they don't do, a fantasy, history, a nature documentary or the inside of a machine is not lived.
> - **Something happens.** Someone in it wants something, and something gets in the way; the mechanism is how it gets solved, or why it goes wrong. That small tension is what makes a viewer want to watch it play out.

**Why:**
- "Vivid, surprising, fun to picture, a world" is what produced the pirates, spies and beekeepers.
- "Lived" and "something happens" ask for what makes people care: recognizing themselves, and wanting to see how it turns out.
- The ranking puts apt and easy first, so interest never wins by being weird.

### 3. The shapes paragraph

**Before (v7)** (Lucas's text from the 3.8 doc):
> Analogies take many shapes. Use whichever fits this concept best: an everyday object or tool; a physical or natural effect (light, water, gravity, heat, growth); an everyday action or routine; a familiar place or system whose parts already work this way (a kitchen, a post office, a library); a game or a sport; nature or the human body as a system; a small situation between characters…

**After (v8):** replaced by the "lived" list above. This is the one place I'm undoing your wording, so it's yours to veto.

**Why:**
- "Nature or the human body as a system" and "a physical or natural effect" are where the cuckoo, the ant trail, the cell's assembly machine and sonar came from.
- Retry 3 ("use a different shape") pushed the writer there on purpose.

### 4. New: whose seat, and how to work

**Before (v7):** none. The writer goes straight to writing five finished candidates.

**After (v8):**
> **Whose seat.** Before you write, decide whose seat the viewer is in for this concept:
> - **the user:** someone using it, who notices its effects: waiting, a wrong answer, something that just works;
> - **the builder:** someone running or building with it, who pays for it, designs it, or gets burned by it;
> - **pure curiosity:** no stakes; the pull is "how does that even work?"
>
> The analogy's main character sits in that seat.
>
> **How to work.**
> 1. Find the click: the one thing about this concept that, once seen, makes it make sense.
> 2. List 12 moments from ordinary life where that same click happens, one line each.
> 3. Judge each against the four qualities above.
> 4. Develop the 5 best.

**Why:**
- Writing five finished candidates in one go produces the five most obvious ones.
- Naming the click first keeps every candidate apt: it must contain the click, not just look like the topic.

### 6. Retries

**Before (v7):**
- Pass 2: "Fix what scored low: each new candidate is more apt, more interesting, and truer to the real mechanism…"
- Pass 3: "Use a different shape of analogy from every earlier candidate…"

**After (v8):**
- Every retry: "These missed for these reasons: [the reasons, in words]. Keep the click. Find moments closer to the viewer's own day, with fewer parts."
- No pass ever asks for a different shape.

## Gap writer

### 1. The goal

**Before (v1):**
> Your goal is engagement. Write the opening question with the most pull for this topic: the one a stranger scrolling past would most want answered, and would stay for, comment on, or send to a friend.

**After (v2):**
> Your goal: the question a viewer in this seat would most want answered, asked about something they have seen, done, or paid for. A curiosity gap is never confusion: the viewer knows exactly what they're missing and wants it.

### 2. The ways in → the seat

**Before (v1):** six ways in, each with no seat:
- the everyday mystery;
- the contradiction;
- the stakes;
- the impossible-seeming number;
- the belief that's wrong;
- "you did this today".

**After (v2):** the analogy's seat decides the angle.
> - **User:** something they notice when they use it. "A site you opened yesterday loads instantly today. What did your phone keep?"
> - **Builder:** something that bites whoever runs it. "Your support bot read the whole manual and still quoted the wrong policy. How?"
> - **Curiosity:** "Type a website's name and it opens in a blink, from anywhere. How does it know where to go?"
>
> Write it as one moment, then the question. Say "you". Use plain words. The question asks about what the viewer sees or pays for, never about the system's insides: the mechanism is the answer, not the question. The line names, in real life, the same kind of problem the analogy's hook shows.

**Why:**
- In the 3.8 set, every line asked an engineer's question ("Why does the system cut the manual into pieces?").
- Every line used 27–35 words in two dense sentences.
- None got above 2.3 on pull.

### 3. Hard constraints

| v1 | v2 draft |
|---|---|
| 1. 3 candidates, each a different way in | 1. 3 candidates, each a different moment, all from the analogy's seat. |
| 2. About the real technology, never the analogy | 2. Same. |
| 3. True | 3. Same. |
| 4. End on the question, no toss | 4. Same. |
| 5. No term, no jargon | 5. Same. |
| 6. 18–36 words | 6. **10–24 words, at most two sentences.** |
| 7. The analogy can answer it: the question is about what the analogy's process shows | 7. **The viewer's question:** about something they see, do or pay for, never "why does the system…". The analogy answers it. |
| 8. Fields | 8. Same, plus `seat`. |
