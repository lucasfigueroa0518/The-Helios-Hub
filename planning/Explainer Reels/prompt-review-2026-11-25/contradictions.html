Why the scripts chose misleading words

Traced from the job folders on the worker: SCRIPT.md, BRIEF.md, the answer key, the reader findings and the fix log for "rule-based system" (job 1aeec7f4) and "artificial intelligence" (job 14103558).

1. The wrong idea was in the source, and every stage treated it as fact

"Hand-written" did not start in the script. The syllabus angle written on 11-22 already said built from hand-written if-then rules, and the 11-24 takeaway said Early AI only followed rules people wrote down, so anything new broke it. From there:

  • The idea generator (v10, told to deliver the takeaway) wrote it into the scope and the payload.
  • The analogy writer mapped it: Every menu choice written by hand → Rules people wrote down one by one. Jev's false-mapping gate judges the mapping against the scope, so it passed.
  • The director must teach BRIEF's lesson frame and may not change what the analogy's parts stand for.
  • The director then wrote the answer key from the same brief: a kind of computer program that follows only rules people wrote down by hand, one by one.
  • The reader test checks the reel against that key, and the fix phase pushes the reel toward the key. Fix 2 made beat 2 say a computer program follows only rules people wrote by hand because the key said so.

No step anywhere asks "is this true the way an expert would say it?" The only accuracy check is circular: the reel is graded against a key written from the same brief.

2. "Plain words" swaps a precise term for a literal one that means something else

To an expert, "hand-written rules" (or "hand-crafted") means rules people wrote explicitly, as code, rather than rules a system learned. To a first-time viewer, "written by hand" means pen and paper, and no code. The plain-words rule (a 12-year-old's words, the term the only technical word) tells the model to swap hard words for easy ones. Nothing tells it the easy word must keep the same meaning. The swap from "hand-coded" to "wrote down by hand" turned a true statement into a false one.

Word budgets make it worse. Beats run about 30 words (the fix log mentions staying inside a 34-word budget), so lines collapse into absolutes: follows only rules people wrote, anything new you gave it broke it.

3. The analogy's own words leak into the script

"Word-perfect" came from the analogy, not from the director. The picked analogy said the tourist can say every listed sentence perfectly, and the director turned that into orders coffee from a phrasebook, word-perfect. It is an idiom, and it points the wrong way: it sounds like fluency, which is the opposite of the point. That reel ran before the 11-24 plain-words rule existed, and even now the rule is self-applied: nothing checks it.

4. The reader test cannot hear what a real newcomer hears

The "newcomer" is a language model told to answer only from the video, with the term swapped for a stand-in word. It still knows every other word. It reads "word-perfect" and "by hand" the way an expert does, so it never flags them, even though its diagnosis prompt lists jargon as a cause to look for. Neither reel's findings mention either phrase.

5. The two reels contradict each other

The AI reel defines AI as software that learns from examples, instead of following steps a programmer wrote. The next reel calls a rule-based system, which is exactly steps a programmer wrote, early AI. Each reel passed its own checks. Nothing compares a reel's claims with the rest of the syllabus or with the field's accepted definitions (rule-based and expert systems are AI; machine learning is one branch of it).

What I think is going on

The pipeline is built to make a reel understood, not to make it true. Every check measures whether the viewer got what the brief meant, and the brief is taken as fact. The plain-words push then rewrites precise terms into everyday words that carry different meanings, and the only reader is a model that understands both versions. Two things are missing: a fact-level check of the brief's claims against how an expert would put them, and a check of each word choice for what it makes a first-timer believe.
