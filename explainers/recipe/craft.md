# Craft: how a film team thinks

This is the expert brain behind every Helios explainer reel. It holds the established craft of
directors, cinematographers, editors, storyboard artists, animators, production designers,
information designers and sound designers, written for any topic, any place and any frame. Read
the sections your role doc names before you plan or build anything.

It is guidance, not a checklist. Each line says what good work does and why; you decide how it
applies to this beat, this shot and this topic, and the picture judges the result (your own stills,
then the picture check). Nothing here is a quota (its numbers are rules of thumb), and nothing here
is a menu to copy.

The rules live elsewhere, each in one place:
- **`minimums.md`**: the eight things every reel must contain, and how a reel fails open.
- **The format** (`story-design.md`): the eight beats, their order and their jobs. It is fixed.
  Your role doc holds your process: what you write, in what order, in which fields.
- **`house-style.md`**: Helios's taste. Where house style has already chosen among the options
  craft allows (the look, the palette and orange, critters and objects, the bookends, pacing, the
  sound bed), follow its choice.

**`frame.md`** lists the tools: what the kit can build and how to call it. It holds no taste.

If two of these ever seem to conflict, minimums win, then the format, then house style, then
craft. Report the conflict in your last message.

## 1. Cinematography

**Shot size: what each one is for**
- Use a wide (establishing) shot when the viewer needs geography, meaning where things are and how
  they relate, before any detail can mean anything. Re-establish whenever the geography changes.
- Use a medium shot to show an action and the body language of whoever does it. It is the
  workhorse "what is happening" size.
- Use a close-up for the one thing that matters most, emotionally or informationally, right now.
  It is the strongest size you have: spend it where the story peaks, never as the default.
- Use an insert (an extreme close shot of an object or detail) to make one detail readable: a dial,
  a label, a part of the mechanism. It also compresses time between other shots.
- Choose each shot's size from what the viewer must learn from it. If two consecutive shots teach
  the same thing, one of them is redundant.

**Progression and contrast between shots**
- Go from orientation to detail (wide, then medium, then close). Each closer shot answers a
  question the wider one raised. A jump straight from very wide to very close usually needs an
  intermediate size or a strong motivating action.
- Make consecutive shots of the same subject clearly different. In our 2.5D world that means a
  dramatic change of size on the same camera angle (next block). Near-identical framings read as
  the subject jumping, not as a new view.
- Use contrast between consecutive shots (size, subject, direction of movement, tone) to raise
  intensity, and affinity (similarity) to calm a passage. Choose deliberately, beat by beat.

**Cutting on the same subject (our 2.5D world)**
- A cut on the same subject keeps the camera angle and changes the size dramatically. Anything
  less reads as a jump cut (`minimums.md` § Jump cuts draws the line).
- Never orbit the camera by small angles around a subject. In a 2.5D world a camera that slides
  30° to one side shows no new view; the world just seems to shift. The camera angle changes only
  when you show a new subject or a new place.
- A cut to a different subject is always free, and it is the usual way to vary shots: a cutaway,
  an insert, the other character, the object being discussed.
- The axial cut (same angle, a much bigger or much smaller size) is your same-subject cut. It works
  when new information or a sharp emotional beat motivates it. A chain of cut-ins on one subject
  that teaches nothing new is stale even when each size change is large: cut to a new subject
  instead.
- A camera move (a push-in or pull-out) is the continuous alternative to a cut-in. Choose the move
  when the shift in attention should feel gradual, the cut when it should feel immediate.

**Axis, screen direction, eyeline**
- Keep the camera on one side of the axis of action (the 180° rule), so left-right relationships
  and the direction of travel stay stable across cuts. Cross the line only on screen (a camera
  move), through a neutral head-on shot, or after a re-establishing wide.
- Hold screen direction for anything that flows (objects, data, characters travelling). If it
  moves left to right in one shot, it keeps doing so in the next, or the viewer reads a reversal.
- Match eyelines. A subject looking off-frame looks toward where its target appears in the next
  shot, with looking room (space in front of the gaze or the motion).

**Angle and what it means**
- Default to eye level: it reads as neutral and objective. Leave it only when a change in power or
  meaning is the point; an angle the audience notices for no reason is a failure.
- A low angle makes a subject feel big, powerful or imposing. A high angle makes it small or
  vulnerable, or gives an overview. A top-down angle shows a system, a layout or a flow like a map.
- Choose objective (observer), subjective (participant, point of view) or over-the-shoulder by
  whose experience the viewer should share at that moment.
- Choose the angle when you establish a subject or a place. Cuts on that same subject keep it.

**Composition**
- Put the subject on a strong point (a third, or dead center for a frontal, iconic statement) and
  keep the most visual contrast there. The eye goes first to the highest tonal or color contrast,
  then to faces and eyes, motion and sharp focus.
- Build depth with foreground, midground and background layers, overlap and diminishing scale. A
  frame with only one plane reads flat and stage-like.
- Use lines in the set (edges, paths, light shafts, gaze, pointing shapes) to lead the eye to the
  subject, never off the frame.
- Keep negative space around the subject so it reads instantly. Empty space is a compositional
  tool, not a gap to fill.

**Camera movement and depth of field**
- Move the camera only for a reason: to follow a moving subject, to reveal new information, or to
  mark an emotional shift. Each move starts and ends on a good composition.
- Keep the camera still when the viewer must read detail or follow a process: camera movement
  competes with on-screen motion for attention.
- Use shallow focus only to isolate one subject from a busy or irrelevant background. Use deep
  focus whenever the relationships between planes matter or several elements must be read. Blur is
  a lens choice, not a style default.
- Never blur anything the voice refers to, and keep the implied lens consistent within a scene:
  blur strength follows distance from the focal plane.

**Vertical 9:16**
- Compose for the tall frame natively. Stack subjects vertically or in depth along the lens axis
  rather than side by side, and favor push-ins and pull-outs over lateral moves.
- Keep key subjects and text inside the safe area, clear of the platform's interface and of our
  caption band (`frame.md` gives the exact bounds).
- Favor closer sizes than you would in 16:9. A true wide shot carries little on a phone, so
  establish quickly, then work in medium and close.

## 2. Lighting

- Motivate every light: it seems to come from a source in the world (the sun, a window, a lamp, a
  screen, a glow). Unmotivated light reads as artificial and flattens the scene.
- Let the key light give shape, from about three-quarters or the side, never flat from the front.
  Its direction is what makes forms read as 3D.
- Set the contrast ratio with the fill, or its absence. Low ratios (about 2:1) feel open,
  optimistic and informational; high ratios (about 8:1) feel dramatic and mysterious. Choose per
  beat.
- Separate the subject from the background with a rim or back light, or with a lighter or darker
  background value. Separation is what makes a silhouette readable.
- Put the brightest value and the highest contrast on the subject, and let the edges and the
  background fall off darker. The eye goes to the brightest area first.
- Use warm and cool color contrast to separate planes and to mark story states, and keep each
  state's color logic the same across the whole reel.
- Add depth with light falloff and atmospheric perspective: distant planes are lighter, lower in
  contrast and less saturated.
- Break up large uniform surfaces with pools of light, practical sources, shadow patterns and the
  materials' own texture. An evenly lit empty room has no focal point and no mood.
- Keep light direction and quality the same across every shot of one scene, so cuts feel like the
  same place and time.

## 3. Editing

- Judge every cut by Murch's Rule of Six, in priority order: emotion (about 51%), story (23%),
  rhythm (10%), eye-trace (7%), the 2D screen plane (5%), 3D space (4%). Give up the lower
  criteria before the higher ones.
- Manage eye-trace: put the incoming shot's point of interest where the viewer's eye already is at
  the end of the outgoing shot. Move it on purpose only when you want a jolt.
- Never cut without a positive reason: new information, a new viewpoint, a reaction, rhythm. Cut
  when a shot has delivered its information and before it goes stale.
- Cut on action: cut mid-movement and continue the motion in the next shot. The movement carries
  the eye and hides the cut.
- A match cut (shape, position, color or motion continued across the cut) links two different
  things, and it is the strongest way to make a link felt, such as the one between the analogy and
  the real mechanism. It is a tool, never a requirement: a plain cut is just as valid when the
  voice makes the link.
- With voiceover, time each cut so the image the words refer to is on screen as the word lands,
  or a few frames before. The picture may lead or trail the sound the way a J-cut or L-cut does;
  it never lands seconds late.
- Let sound (music, an effect, the voice itself) bridge across cuts and scene changes, so the edit
  feels continuous even when the image changes completely.
- Vary shot length with information load: hold longer on a new or complex image until it can be
  read, and cut faster through simple or repeated material. Uniform durations feel like a
  slideshow.
- Cut where a thought completes. The moment an idea resolves is the moment a viewer would blink.
- When unsure of the exact frame, cut a little long rather than short. A shot cut before it
  registers is wasted.

## 4. Storyboarding

- Write the shot list before drawing. Each shot states its purpose (what the viewer learns or
  feels), its size, angle, subject and duration. A shot without a stated purpose gets cut.
- Put one idea in each shot. If you can't describe the panel in one short sentence, split it or
  simplify it.
- Stage each idea so it is completely and unmistakably clear: one center of interest, nothing
  competing with it, the camera placed where the action reads best.
- Test readability in silhouette. The subject and its action are recognizable as a flat black
  shape, with no overlapping limbs or props hiding the key shape.
- Board every action the voice describes on the words that describe it, trips and results
  included (temporal contiguity, § 6).
- Block in depth: arrange the subjects and the camera so the relationship being taught (cause and
  effect, before and after, part and whole) appears as a spatial relationship in the frame.
- Set up visual motifs (a shape, a color, a prop, a framing) and bring them back in a changed form
  at the payoff. Recurrence makes a short piece feel designed and helps memory.
- Plan the visual intensity curve across the whole reel with contrast and affinity: calmer and more
  alike early, more contrast at the climax, resolution at the end.
- Keep sets, props and characters consistent across shots (scale, color, position), so the world
  reads as one continuous place.
- Board the transitions between scenes along with the scenes. How one image becomes the next is
  part of the story.

## 5. Animation

The twelve principles of animation, applied to simple characters and objects:

- **Squash and stretch:** let things deform on impact and acceleration while keeping their volume,
  so they feel like they have mass and material.
- **Anticipation:** precede each significant action with a small opposite move (a wind-up, a
  lean), so the viewer knows something is about to happen and where to look.
- **Staging:** present one action at a time, from the clearest angle, against an uncluttered
  background.
- **Pose to pose:** design the key poses first, each a clear, readable statement, then the
  in-betweens. The key poses are what the viewer remembers.
- **Follow-through and overlap:** parts don't all stop at once. Appendages, antennae and loose
  parts settle after the main body.
- **Slow in and slow out:** ease into and out of key poses. Use linear motion only for mechanical
  or constant-speed things.
- **Arcs:** natural motion travels on curves, so move along arcs unless the thing is a machine on
  a track.
- **Secondary action:** add a small supporting action that reinforces the main one, never one that
  competes with it.
- **Timing and spacing:** timing (how many frames) sets duration and weight; spacing (how far apart
  the positions are) sets the feel of speed. Close spacing reads slow and heavy, wide spacing fast.
- **Exaggeration, solid drawing, appeal:** push poses past realism so they read at small size, keep
  forms solid and consistent in 3D, and design with simple, clear shapes that are pleasant to
  watch.
- **Acting from intent:** every gesture comes from what the character wants and is thinking.
  Thought comes before action, so show the decision (a look, a beat) before the move.
- **Holds:** keep a character's or a hero object's held pose subtly alive with a moving hold (a
  slight drift or a breath), so the frame never feels dead. Information elements are different:
  they stay still until they change (§ 10).
- **Choreography:** when several things move, lead with the hero, stagger the rest, and limit
  simultaneous motion so attention has one path.

## 6. Explainer craft and Mayer

**What the best explainers do**
- Decide each scene's visual metaphor in a sketch phase, before any illustration. The metaphor
  carries the idea; rendering comes after.
- Let the recorded voiceover set the timing; the animation follows it.
- Within a beat, go concrete before abstract: show a specific case working before you generalize
  it. Across beats, the format fixes the order.
- Motivate before defining: show the problem or question that makes the mechanism necessary, then
  show the mechanism.
- Show a process as the same on-screen objects changing state (moving, splitting, combining,
  recoloring). Don't swap in a new diagram for each step: continuity of objects is what makes a
  transformation understandable.
- Give each concept one persistent visual identity (shape, color, icon) and keep it for the whole
  reel, across the analogy and the mechanism. Color coding works only if it never changes meaning.
  (This is also a minimum: `minimums.md` 6.)
- Build progressively: start with the simplest version of the system on screen and add one
  component per beat. Never show the full system first.
- Keep one metaphor through a section and map its parts explicitly to the real parts. Mixing
  metaphors makes the viewer re-learn the world.
- Simplify ruthlessly: remove every element that doesn't serve the mechanism being explained, so
  attention lands on the core.
- Animate charts and diagrams one element at a time, highlighting what the voice is discussing and
  dimming the rest.

**Learning science (Mayer's multimedia principles)**
- **Coherence:** leave out extraneous words, pictures, sounds and decoration. Anything that carries
  no meaning costs attention.
- **Signaling:** cue what matters (highlight, color, motion, arrows, a camera move toward it) so the
  viewer sees how the essential material is organized.
- **Redundancy:** narration plus graphics beats narration plus graphics plus the same words as
  on-screen text. Limit text to a few key terms or labels.
- **Spatial contiguity:** put a label right next to the thing it names, never in a separate legend
  or panel (§ 10 says how).
- **Temporal contiguity:** show the picture at the same moment the words describe it, as they
  describe it. This has the largest effect of all of them, and narrating an action over an image
  that doesn't show it is the most common words-and-pictures failure. (This is also a minimum:
  `minimums.md` 4.)
- **Segmenting:** break the explanation into small, clearly bounded steps with a beat between them.
- **Pre-training:** introduce the names and the look of the key parts before showing them interact.
- **Modality:** explain with spoken words plus pictures, not written words plus pictures. The eye
  can't read and watch at once.

## 7. Script and voiceover

- The voiceover comes first. Its priorities, in order: an engaging story, teaching effectively,
  communicating at a high level. Write it and settle it before you plan shots: it is the timing
  spine every shot is planned against.
- Plan in two columns (audio | visual), so every line of the voice has a planned image. Where a
  line can't be shown well, look for a better picture first. Change the wording only a little, and
  only where the line keeps its story and its teaching.
- Hook in the first seconds with a specific unresolved question, tension or promise, and make the
  first frame readable without sound.
- Pay the hook off progressively: each beat gives part of the answer, so the payoff completes it.
  An answer withheld until the last seconds feels manipulative.
- Connect beats with "but" and "therefore", never "and then". Causal links make a mechanism feel
  like a story.
- Use a story spine: the normal state, a disruption or problem, the step-by-step consequence, the
  resolution. It gives even a technical explanation narrative pull.
- Write for the ear: short sentences, concrete nouns and active verbs, one idea per sentence. Each
  sentence becomes a shot.
- Don't spend words on what the picture already shows. Say why it happens or what it means, so
  words and image complement each other instead of duplicating.
- Budget about 140–160 spoken words a minute. Dense voiceover leaves the visuals no time to teach:
  where an image must land, make room for it in the writing (a short sentence, a line that lets
  the picture carry the beat). Pauses themselves stay short (`house-style.md` § Captions, voice
  and pacing).

## 8. Production design

- Decide what the place must tell the viewer (where we are, what happens here, who uses it) before
  designing anything. The set is a storytelling tool: the viewer should answer "where am I?" at a
  glance.
- Choose the kind of setting from what the beat needs, not from habit: an interior for a bounded
  task; an exterior or a landscape for scale and open systems; a journey between places for
  transport, progress or steps over time; an abstract or tabletop stage when the idea has no
  natural location. The kind of space decides which actions and camera moves are
  possible.
- Treat a stylized stage (a diorama, a tabletop, a floating platform, a miniature world) as a full
  set with its own floor, edges, light and props. "Abstract" still needs a ground plane, depth and
  a reason for the light.
- Show use and history with cause-and-effect dressing (wear, traces, something left mid-task, tools
  where they were used). Evidence lets the viewer infer the story without narration.
- Dress in a hierarchy: big masses and silhouettes first, then a few readable mid-size props, then
  fine detail only near the focal point. Detail pulls the eye, so it belongs where the eye should
  go.
- Cut every element that doesn't say where, what or who. Never add something only because it looks
  cool; an empty set and a cluttered set fail the same test.
- Give the environment a shape language that matches its role (round and soft for safe and
  friendly, rectangular for stable or institutional, angular and diagonal for tension or danger)
  and keep it consistent across the world. Shape carries feeling before detail is read.
- Establish scale with set pieces everyone knows the size of (a door, a chair, a counter, a step)
  plus repeated elements that shrink with depth. Size means nothing without a reference.
- Make a color script for the whole reel before final frames: one small color key per beat,
  showing how palette, value and temperature shift with the arc. Color progression is designed at
  the sequence level, not frame by frame. Tie a color to a story element and bring it back
  wherever that element matters (one identity per concept, § 6).
- Plan depth along with color: flatter, shallower staging for constrained or low beats, deeper
  space for open or high beats. Spatial depth is a second emotional dial.
- Design the value structure first: reduce each frame to 3–4 value masses with the subject on the
  strongest contrast. Value makes a frame read, hue doesn't; a frame that only works in color has
  no structure.
- Never build a one-value, one-hue world. Give each set a dominant hue family, one accent and a real
  range from light to dark. An all-white set has no depth, no focal point and no mood.
- Stylize to a sweet spot: fewer, larger shapes, details that don't face the camera removed, one
  consistent surface treatment everywhere. Too real looks like live action; too bare looks cheap.
- Lock the world's rules (floor plan, palette, light direction, materials, scale) and never break
  them between shots: a broken rule makes the viewer feel cheated. Vary spaces only on purpose
  (cool to warm, tight to open) to mark progression.

## 9. Character and prop design

- Design the silhouette first. A character or prop must be recognizable as a solid black shape at
  thumbnail size before color or detail is read.
- Check the read order: silhouette, then value and color, then detail. If a design needs color or
  detail to be recognized, its shape is too weak.
- Give each thing you design (a hero object, a prop, an outfit's silhouette) one dominant shape
  tied to its personality (a circle for friendly and soft, a square for stable or stubborn, a
  triangle for dynamic or threatening), with secondary shapes only for contrast. Shape states
  personality instantly.
- Vary size and proportion inside a design (one big, one medium, one small mass). Equal-sized
  parts look dull and read poorly at small size.
- A critter's face is what the viewer relates to: eyes, brows and a mouth, simplified toward the
  features that carry expression, never away from them. A simple face invites the viewer to
  identify with it. Objects have no face (house style); they carry feeling through motion (below).
- Make expressions read at phone size. The kit draws the face; you choose the mood and frame the
  critter big enough that its gaze and brows read (the brows carry the emotion, the pupils show
  where it looks). Test each expression at about 10% of the frame.
- Pose with one clear line of action, the limbs separated from the body in silhouette, and no
  symmetrical "twinning". A pose the viewer must decode reads as weird; a pose that reads as a
  shape reads as intent.
- State a role at a glance with one iconic costume piece or held tool, not a detailed outfit. One
  strong signifier reads faster than many small ones.
- Build appeal from clarity, coherent proportions and a hint of personality, not prettiness.
  Appeal is what makes a simple design pleasant to watch for a whole reel.
- A hero object stays a pure object: its personality comes only from motion (timing, anticipation,
  squash and stretch, a lean or a tilt toward what it wants). Commit to that fully; an object that
  half-acts like a character reads as neither. (House style gives objects no face.)
- Cheat scale for readability: draw the props the action depends on bigger than real-world
  proportion, and shrink or drop the rest. A prop has to read on a phone, not be measured.
- Design props in the same shape language, edge softness and level of detail as the characters.
  Mismatched rendering makes a prop look pasted in from another world.
- Contrast the hero against the set in shape, value and color. A hero that matches its background
  disappears.
- Lock each design once, in the world module (it is the reel's model sheet: looks, proportions,
  colors, sizes relative to each other), and build every shot from it. Consistency across shots
  needs a reference, not memory.

## 10. Information design

For the frames that show the real mechanism.

- Give each frame one focal point and an explicit reading order (primary, secondary, ambient), and
  run the squint test: if the hero isn't obvious when the frame is blurred, the hierarchy is weak.
- Make every mark carry information. Remove frames, card borders, extra shadows, gradients and
  ornaments that encode nothing: non-data ink competes with the data. (The house look's soft
  shading of a real object is that object's material, not ornament.)
- Don't let UI chrome (cards, tiles, buttons, windows, dashboard panels) stand in for an idea. Draw
  the thing itself, or a pictogram of it, and show the relationship (flow, containment,
  transformation). A box labeled with a concept shows only that the concept exists, not how it
  works.
- Choose the diagram form from the question the beat answers: who or what, a portrait or an icon;
  how much, a count or a bar; where, a map; when, a timeline; how, a flow; why, cause and effect.
  For a mechanism, prefer flow, before and after, comparison, and part and whole.
- Show structure with Gestalt instead of boxes: proximity to group, similarity (same shape or color
  means same kind), connecting lines for relationships, common fate (things that move together
  belong together). Spacing and motion group things without adding ink.
- Separate layers by contrast: context and structure (axes, containers, guides, the inactive
  parts) faint and low in contrast, the active elements strong. Heavy structure creates noise.
- Use color sparingly, as code: a muted neutral base with small areas of strong accent for
  "active" or "important" (house style says which color marks the focus). Bright color works best
  in small spots on quiet ground.
- Keep new information to about one element per voice clause and a few groups on screen at once
  (around four meaningful chunks). Working memory holds only a handful of new items.
- Show the mechanism big: a few large things at a confident scale, with what they connect to and
  where they sit, never a few small icons on blank ground. Fill the frame by composition and
  scale, never by adding things; empty space must frame something.
- Build one icon system and keep it for the whole reel: one grid, one stroke weight, one corner
  radius, one style (filled or outline), one palette, and one icon per concept (§ 6).
- Compare with repetition: same design, same scale, only the variable changes (small multiples, or
  repeated units for quantity). The eye sees a difference only against something held constant.
- Animate the mechanism, not the entrance. Motion shows travel, transformation, splitting,
  combining, or cause and effect. Information elements stay still until something happens to
  them: cut decorative bounces, wiggles and idle loops. Motion is the strongest attention cue and
  must carry meaning.
- Label directly on or beside the thing, in 1–3 words, horizontal and high in contrast, with no
  legends or keys. The eye shouldn't have to travel to decode.
- Set type for the phone: the house faces only, at most two or three sizes as hierarchy, large
  (well above the readable minimum in `frame.md`), short lines, inside the safe area, and key
  terms only, never sentences that duplicate the voice.

## 11. Sound

You place the sounds: where each lands and what it is. Code chooses and levels the music, sets the
mix and keeps effects under the voice.

- Plan sound with the picture. Before you animate a shot, read its action and its `why:` and list
  the events a sound will hit (the sync points); each becomes an event on its landing frame.
  Sound designed with the picture lands; sound added last only decorates.
- Use synchresis: a sound placed exactly on a visual event fuses with it and gives it weight,
  material and reality, so put hits on contacts, appearances, snaps and state changes. Keep them
  frame-accurate. Viewers notice audio that leads by more than about 45 ms or lags by more than
  about 125 ms (broadcast practice holds +40/−60 ms), so land on the frame or at most one frame
  late.
- Sync every event that carries meaning (house style sets how dense: `house-style.md` § Sound and
  music). What to avoid is decorative syncing. Sound on motion that means nothing (idle loops,
  wiggles, flourishes) is noise, and it weakens the hits that matter. Literal sync on every
  twitch (Mickey-mousing) belongs only to a deliberate comic gag.
- Give each material its own foley family (paper, pen, wood, plastic, glass, cloth) and keep it the
  same all the way through. Tactile, hand-made sounds make a stylized world feel physical, and
  consistent pairs teach the viewer what things are made of.
- Give each recurring concept or state a short, distinct sound (arrives, succeeds, fails,
  transforms), keep it fixed, and vary it only as the concept changes. A fixed sound-meaning pair
  works like fixed color coding. Each frame is built on its own, so describe that concept's sound
  in the same words in every frame; the library then returns the same family.
- Treat the voice as the priority layer: it carries the meaning and must stay intelligible. Let the
  densest clusters of effects fall between phrases.
- Build density without mud: spread layers across kinds (voice, foley, music) rather than stacking
  several sounds of the same kind at once. Listeners track only about two to three layers of one
  kind (Murch's "rule of two and a half").
- Bridge cuts with sound: let a sound start just before a cut or ring past it, use a whoosh only
  for real travel or a camera move, and mark a change of place with a change of sound (that
  place's own foley family).
- Use silence as emphasis: hold the effects back for a beat before a key reveal, so its sound, or
  the line, lands.
- Cut on the voice and the action, not on the music's beat. Cutting on every beat becomes
  predictable and lets the music drive the story.
- Keep UI and motion-graphic sounds short, soft and from one family, and scale each sound to the
  size of its motion. A decorative entrance (something appearing for no reason the viewer needs)
  stays silent; an entrance that means something gets its sound.
- Avoid pitched, chordal effects that clash with the music. Choose non-tonal textures (paper,
  clicks, taps): musical effects fight the score.
- The music leaves room for the voice: instrumental, its groove carried by drums and bass, sparse
  in the midrange where speech sits, no lyrics. Code searches for that and levels it.
