# Explainers 4.1 — the director's principles

Read this before anything else. It is what an animation director teaches a new team: how to
think, not a list of cases. Every reel is a new topic, a new place and a new cast, so nothing here
names a kitchen, a hospital or a pose. When a rule elsewhere seems to conflict with a principle
here, the principle wins and the conflict is a bug to report.

The test of every frame: **a stranger watching on a phone with the sound on understands what
happened, who did it and why it matters, without reading a label.** Labels confirm; the picture
explains.

## 1. Set design: a place you could walk into

- **Recognizable in one second.** Before drawing, name the 3–5 things that make this place *this
  place* to a stranger (an ER: the curtain bays, the triage desk, the wall monitor; a kitchen: the
  counter run, the window over the sink, the fridge). Build those first, at true size.
- **Architecture, not a floor in a void.** At least a floor and two walls meeting at a corner, or
  a wall with an opening, so the space has a shape. Openings (doors, windows, pass-throughs) give
  depth and a source of light, and show what lies beyond.
- **Three depth layers.** Foreground (one piece close to the lens, partly cropped: frames the
  shot and sells depth), midground (where the action stands), background (walls, openings and
  what is seen through them). Every shot has all three unless it is an insert.
- **Materials and color.** Each surface has a material (tile, wood, painted plaster, steel,
  linoleum) drawn with its own ramp and a little pattern where the real thing has one. Pick a
  color story for the place: two or three hues from the palette that the real place has, in
  quiet values. The set is rich in detail and calm in contrast; orange and the brightest light
  stay with the read.
- **Inhabited, not decorated.** Dress the set with what this place really holds, at its real
  density (a kitchen has a kettle, a fruit bowl, a towel on the oven door). Nothing generic, no
  random plants or posters. The dressing never competes with the read: it is quieter, smaller,
  or out of focus behind it.
- **A place beyond.** When the story goes somewhere (a store, another room, a server far away),
  that somewhere exists: seen through an opening, cut to as its own set, or both.

## 2. Acting: every pose is an action

- **Pose from intent.** A critter is always *doing* something with a target: reaching for,
  holding, looking at, pointing toward, recoiling from, waiting for. Write the intent; the rig
  solves the arms, the lean and the gaze. If you cannot say what a pose is doing, it is wrong.
- **Weight and balance.** The body leans into effort and away from fear; weight sits over the
  foot it stands on; a carried thing pulls the body. Nothing is stiff and symmetrical except a
  deliberate freeze.
- **Eyes lead.** The head and eyes turn to a target before the hands move. The viewer looks where
  the critter looks.
- **Hands hold things.** Anything carried is in a hand, every frame. Two held things use two
  hands. Put a thing down before picking up the next.
- **Faces are always whole.** Every visible face has eyes, brows and a mouth, and its mood fits
  the moment. A neutral face is calm, not blank.
- **Clear silhouettes.** A gesture reads in silhouette: arms away from the body, never across the
  eyes or the face. Pain, effort and joy are whole-body poses, not only eyebrows.
- **Costume says the job before any label.** Pick the one or two garments a stranger associates
  with the role (an apron with a tie and a pocket for a cook; a white coat for a doctor). Avoid
  colors and shapes that belong to another job.

## 3. Cast: who is on screen

- **Few on screen, one to watch.** At most three critters in a shot read as individuals, and only
  the ones the moment needs. The rest are off screen, not squeezed in. When the idea itself is a
  crowd (many workers at once), the crowd is one subject: deep in the background or in its own
  shot, all doing the same action, smaller and quieter than the read, never mixed in with the
  individuals.
- **Never a body part alone.** A hand never enters a shot without its critter. An insert either
  shows the object by itself (it moves, or is placed by something we just saw), or is framed from
  the side so the critter that does the action is in the picture with its face whole.
- **Introduce the hero alone.** When the voice says "this <role>", the hero is the clear subject:
  alone in the shot, or lit, centred and acting while anyone else is still or out of focus.
- **Never overlap to fit.** If the cast does not fit, change the shot (wider, a new angle, fewer
  people), never stack critters on top of each other.

## 4. Cinematography: every cut earns its place

- **A cut changes what the viewer learns**: a new subject, or the same subject from a clearly new
  angle (at least about 30° around it, or a change of height). Never cut from one view to a
  zoomed copy of the same view: that is a jump cut and reads as a glitch. Judge it by the
  picture, not the numbers: if two consecutive shots look alike at a glance (same subject, same
  side, similar size), it is a jump even when the camera moved.
- **Props read as what they are.** A small object that carries the idea (a plug, a card, a pill)
  has the one shape detail a stranger knows it by (prongs, a chip and stripe, a coating), large
  enough to see on a phone.
- **Within a shot, at most one motivated move**: a push in as tension builds, a pan to follow a
  walk, a pull out to reveal. Otherwise the camera is still.
- **Shot sizes have jobs.** Wide: where are we, who is where. Medium: what are they doing. Close:
  how do they feel. Insert: the one small thing that matters. Open each scene wide enough to read
  the place.
- **Depth of field is a choice, not a filter.** Keep everything sharp unless the shot has a reason
  to isolate one thing (an insert, a reveal, a rack focus from one read to the next). Never blur
  the thing the voice is naming.
- **Frame for the phone.** The subject and every label sit inside the safe area for the whole
  shot, including during moves. Nothing important is cropped by the frame edge.
- **Labels belong to things.** A label sits on its entity and leaves when the entity leaves.

## 5. Show what the voice says

- **Every action the voice describes happens on screen**, in order, at the moment it is said.
  "Goes to the store" shows the store or the trip (a cut to the destination, or the door closing,
  time passing and the critter coming back changed). Walking to a door and back is not a trip.
- **Every result is visible.** If something is poured, it fills; if something is fixed, we see it
  fixed; if something spoils, we see the spoilage, not a stripe changing color.
- **Keep the mapping straight.** The definition, the analogy and the example name the same parts
  the same way (if the fridge is the cache, the carton is a cached copy, everywhere).
- **The payoff resolves.** The last beat shows the answer working, the satisfying result, never
  the opening tension again.

## 6. Explaining the real tech

Before building any tech beat, decide *how this idea is best shown*, and write the choice and the
reason in one line at the top of the frame module. Choose from techniques, not layouts:

| Technique | Use when the idea is… |
|---|---|
| Process / flow | a thing moving through steps (a request, a packet, a token) |
| Before / after | a change in speed, cost, size or quality |
| Side-by-side comparison | two options or paths with different outcomes |
| Transformation | one thing becoming another (text into tokens, words into vectors) |
| Scale | a size, count or cost that is surprising |
| Structure | parts arranged in a system (layers, a network, a stack) |
| Timeline | order and waiting over time |
| Cause and effect | an input that triggers a consequence |
| Match cut | the analogy object becoming its tech twin, to bridge the two worlds |
| Product at work | the real app or screen doing the job, with real content |

- **Show the mechanism moving**, not boxes with names. Data visibly travels, piles up, splits,
  waits, gets copied, gets compared. A card with a title is a last resort.
- **Real content.** Real-looking messages, files, numbers and screens, short enough to read.
- **One identity per concept.** Each concept keeps one icon, one color and one shape across every
  frame (the reel's identity map). Highlight with light or a border, never by recoloring it.
- **Nothing empty.** A container appears with its content, never seconds before it.
- **Readable on a phone.** Few words, large type, strong contrast.

## 7. Look at your own work

You can render stills of your frame. Do it at every shot change and at the end of every frame,
look at them as a stranger would, and fix what fails before you hand off:

1. Do I know where we are?
2. Do I know who the named character is?
3. Does every pose read as an action? Is every face whole?
4. Is anything floating, cut off, overlapping or crowded?
5. Does the picture show exactly what the voice says right now?
6. Would this cut feel like a jump?
7. Does the tech beat show the idea moving, or only label it?

Stop after the revisions your role allows; report anything you could not fix.
