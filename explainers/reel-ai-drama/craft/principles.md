# Reel AI Drama — the writing principles

A Reel AI Drama is a true story from the AI world, told by a narrator over real pictures in about a minute. You
already know how to tell a good story; these are the principles this format holds you to. They describe what a good
script does, not a shape it must take: the story decides its own structure.

The musts are HARD CONSTRAINTS: a script that breaks one is wrong. The principles are craft: weigh them together
and use judgment.

## Musts (HARD CONSTRAINTS)

1. **The first line is the hook, and it grabs.** Nothing comes before it, and no title or label comes with it. It is
   one short line of at most 10 spoken words. From its first words, a stranger scrolling past cares and needs what
   comes next (the hook principles below). Never spend it on a bare date: the next line can say the time
   frame. A time tied to something the
   viewer already knows is not a bare date, and may carry the consequence. [MD-115, MD-118, MD-34, MD-134]
2. **Facts only from the facts.** Every claim, name, number, date and quote comes from the facts you were given and
   lists the fact ids it uses. Quotes are verbatim. Never invent, combine, embellish or round a figure into a
   different claim. The hook's framing may dramatize (a tease, a provocation, a loaded question, a tie to today) as long as
   everything it asserts is true. [MD-117]
3. **Length and pause.** Aim for the length the user message gives: about 60 seconds. Go up to 80 only when the
   story can't be told in 60; the extra time is for a story that needs it, never room to fill. At most one pause,
   of about 0.15 seconds. [MD-103]
4. **Credit only what needs it.** Settled facts stand on their own. A fact marked "(disputed)" gives both sides. A
   fact marked "(one source)" and any allegation rest on their source: the narration names that source once in the
   whole reel, where the viewer first leans on it (never on screen), and every later fact from it stands on its own,
   however many carry the mark. A source is named again only where a dispute needs both sides. [MD-74, MD-126]
5. **The close question.** One question of about six words plays over the brand close after the last line. It
   follows from how the story ended and is never a line of narration. [MD-05, MD-18]
6. **Few players.** One person to follow from start to finish, one opposing force, and about two new names in the
   whole reel. A known name is free; every other person, company or product named costs the viewer attention and
   counts. Anyone past that is a plain role or is cut. Unnamed people count too: each person or group the viewer
   has to keep track of, named or not, is a player, so fold extras into the opposing side or leave them out.
   Keep the person the viewer follows at the centre to the end, rather than handing the story to someone new
   midway. A person carries the story, never a
   company or product. Every stranger gets a plain handle the moment they appear, tied to something the viewer
   already knows where one exists.

## Principles

### The story

1. **Find the main tension first:** what is at stake, between whom or what, and why it mattered then. Every line
   serves it.
2. **Tell one episode:** a short span around that tension. Anything much later is mentioned briefly, and only when
   it is important to situate the story against present-day reality.
3. **Let the larger forces shaping AI run through the story as stakes,** early, never as a closing lesson: the
   story's weight comes from what it put at risk. [D-6]
4. **Centre only the few people the story can carry** (must 6). Anyone else gets their one act, with no side story.
   [MD-75]
5. **Know the last line before you write the first;** the ending is where the hook's promise is paid.

### Orientation

6. **Set the scene before the action,** as a scene, not a roll call: when, where, who is there and what they are to
   each other, so the viewer is never lost.
7. **Place every new part relative to one already set:** a person by their tie to someone the viewer has met, a
   place by where it sits from one already named, an event by its time from one already told. [MD-95]
8. **Say who someone was then** when the viewer knows them as something else now; named, the gap is a hook.
9. **Motive before action:** before someone acts, the viewer knows what they want and why.
10. **Deliver context just in time:** the beat before it is needed, after the question that makes it wanted, and as
    something a person uses, risks or fights over.

### Tension

11. **Each line follows from the one before.** Claim a cause only when the facts give one; otherwise say what
    happened next.
12. **Keep the viewer leaning forward:** raise a question before you answer it, hold the answer back, let the
    stakes grow. Withhold how and why, and the outcome unless the hook gave it, never orientation.
13. **Make the viewer care before you make them worry:** danger to a stranger produces no suspense.
14. **Let the pressure act:** whatever stands in the way (a person, a rival, a deadline, money, the odds) does
    something the viewer sees.
15. **Show the cost:** what each choice risks or loses is what makes the outcome matter.
16. **Pay every setup and set up every payoff:** a surprise should feel inevitable looking back.
17. **Something new every few seconds:** a fact, a stake, a move, a shift. Never a line that only restates.

### The hook

The viewer decides in a second and a half whether to stay. The hook earns that by making them care, a lot, at
once. [MD-115]

18. **The biggest real consequence, carried by a name they know.** Lead with the largest thing the story actually
    changed, the one a stranger would care about without knowing the story, and let a person or a company the viewer
    knows carry it. An event that only matters once you know the story (a routine move, an internal cost) is not a
    hook; it comes later, as the way the consequence happened. Put the words where it hits early in the line,
    within its first seven: the opening's picture turns on them, and it must turn within 2.5 seconds. [MD-134]
19. **One question the viewer could say back.** Open one clear question the viewer could repeat in a sentence and
    feels close to answering; a vague mystery gives nothing to chase. A statement that makes the viewer ask it
    usually beats asking it out loud; a spoken question is allowed, rarely the strongest. [MD-121]
20. **Cut against what they believe.** Turn on a contradiction with what the viewer believes today about that
    person, company or idea.
21. **Make it theirs.** Tie the stakes to the viewer's life now: the tools they use, their data, their work, the AI
    world they know. A stake that would fit any AI story isn't one.
22. **Keep the consequence true to size.** State what actually happened, not what might have, and claim no more of
    it than the facts bear.
23. **Charged part first.** Lead with the conflict, the cost, the secret or the reversal; the date, the place and
    titles wait for the next line.
24. **Give away or tease the outcome, whichever pulls harder.** A known ending turns the question into how and
    why. [MD-90]
25. **One exact detail.** Let one concrete detail carry it; specifics read as true and belong to this story alone.
26. **A strong feeling.** Aim for awe, anger, anxiety or amusement over calm interest.
27. **Promise only what the reel pays.** The ending pays the hook's promise; a broken promise loses the viewer.
28. **One promise, two ways.** The spoken line and the first picture make one promise, and through the captions it
    works on mute.
29. **Clear on one cold read.** No insider term as the word the hook turns on; never open on "There is", a pronoun
    or an unexplained name.

### The ending

30. **Land it:** the ending pays off the tension the hook opened, then stops: on an act or a thread still open, never
    a summary, a wrap-up signal or a new conflict.
31. **Ask a close question viewers would split on,** one the story gave both sides of, never one whose answer is
    obvious.

### The telling

32. **Specific beats general:** dates, the concrete detail of the moment, the words people really said.
33. **Use few numbers,** rounded only where rounding stays true. Compare figures on one base and say what the gap
    means; never make the viewer do arithmetic.
34. **A natural storyteller's voice:** connected sentences you would say to a friend, one breath each, in the past
    tense, steady and unperformed. Let the facts carry the feeling; never call anything shocking. No fragments,
    drumroll colons or one-word sentences. [MD-93]
35. **Write for the ear:** a listener can't re-read. Name people with their role the first time and call each by one
    name throughout; repeat the key noun rather than a synonym; keep each "he" or "she" unmistakable. When a name
    could be heard as ordinary words, say what it is first.
36. **A stranger with little AI background can follow every line.**
37. **Write toward pictures that exist:** favor moments the visual inventory can show.
